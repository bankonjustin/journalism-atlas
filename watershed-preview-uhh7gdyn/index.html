A Watershed Moment for News Deserts

Medill's new map shows which counties have lost local papers. Look closer and information still flows across county lines and signs of journalism spring up underneath.

On a weekday in Harrisburg, Illinois, you can still learn a lot about your county. The sheriff posts. A heat advisory lands on your phone. The funeral home puts up the obituaries. What's harder to learn is why: why the county board voted the way it did, what the school budget means, whether anyone is asking. Harrisburg's paper, the Daily Register, printed its last edition in June, one of [13] Illinois papers Paddock Publications closed.

Medill's State of Local News report counts 238 counties like this one, with "no locally based source of news"—26 more than a year ago. That count should worry anyone who cares about self-government. It worries me.

This year, Medill also looked at independent journalist creators for the first time. Its own Chicago survey found close to half of younger residents get local news from creators every day. To see who's making it, its researchers turned to the Independent Journalism Atlas, drawing on the roughly 1,500 creator-journalists we'd mapped as of May. They found that "less than 10% of this sample is covering local issues about their community," and that two-thirds of those do original reporting. Our own data from that period says the same thing. They're also right that finding these people is hard—the platforms don't make it easy.

That snapshot did us a favor. It sent us back to look harder, and the map has moved fast since: the roughly 1,500 creator-journalists in the Atlas in May are now more than 2,400. In June we went deep on Chicago, and there the categories start to blur. We've mapped 43 independent journalists on local beats like city hall, schools and neighborhoods. Thirteen of them already appear in Medill's own list of local outlets. So the useful question isn't whether someone is a creator or an outlet; it's whether they're doing the work. And here's the trouble: counts see mastheads, money follows counts, and the people doing the work in the thinnest places show up in neither.

I've spent twenty years inside newsrooms—the Washington Post, the New York Times, NPR—and watched the aggregation era burn publishers to the ground. At the ATmosphere protocol conference in March, I argued that "desert" is the wrong word for what's left. Penny Abernathy put the term on the map with her work at UNC, which made the loss impossible to ignore. Yet a desert suggests a place with nothing in it. These places have signs of journalistic life well beyond newspapers.

A better metaphor is a watershed. Water doesn't stop at a county line. It flows everywhere, runs off the surface, sinks underground and comes up wherever someone digs. Information moves the same way. In these counties it's plentiful on the surface: the sheriff's posts, the alerts, the Facebook pages, the radio station one county over. Most of it runs off. It tells you what happened and almost never why. Fifteen years ago, Steve Waldman's report for the FCC warned that this kind of abundance is an "illusion of bounty" masking a shortage of reporting. These places don't lack information. They have information without journalism. What's scarce is the digging: someone who goes to the meeting, reads the minutes and checks the claim.

But in more places than the desert map shows, someone has picked up a shovel to do that digging and turn information into journalism.

A few terms carry the rest of this piece. The surface is what anyone can see: posts, alerts, obituaries. It tells you what happened, then runs off. Digging is journalism: going to the meeting, reading the minutes, checking the claim. Hand-dug wells are newsrooms that neighbors rebuild after a paper dies or hollows out. Springs are people who came up on their own, outside any institution. The picks and shovels are the money and tools that keep the digging going. And the basin is the whole shared system, which no one can tend without a map.

Hand-Dug Wells

The first group picks up where a paper left off. Most were in the room when it died, or watched it hollow out: the editor, the reporter, the reader who showed up to the last-ditch meeting. Their tools are modest—a nonprofit filing, a newsletter platform, a handful of volunteers. Their financial runway is short. And what they make ranges from a community bulletin board to a county paper with four bylines in a single issue.

In Anaconda, Montana, the Leader printed its final paper on June 5, after 56 years. Days later the town had its second fatal public shooting in a year. "It feels like the heart and soul of the community just got scattered," James Rosien, who had edited the paper since 2011, told Montana Public Radio. By July he and a group of neighbors had formed a nonprofit to start another one. They named it the Anaconda Weekly Review, after the town's first newspaper from the 1880s, and they began publishing this August.

In Brandon, Vermont, about 60 people came to a meeting in September 2025 to try to save the Brandon Reporter, a weekly of roughly 30 years. It closed anyway. By February six of them were publishing the Neshobe Current. Look at one week of it: a selectboard story on a new antislavery trail, surprising sludge costs and paying off the police station bond; who's funding the local state rep races; the WWII dead of Brandon High School; the town's fire district; an artist of the week; a "Did you know?" from the public library. "We're more of a community newsletter than a source of original news," volunteer Jeff Weston told Vermont Public. Jeff is being modest. Six neighbors explaining a sludge bill is the work.

In Owen County, Indiana, the paper didn't close. It thinned out. Since 2019, under GateHouse and then Gannett, the Spencer Evening World has shrunk to a weekly with much of its content coming from outside the county—what Abernathy calls a ghost newspaper. One of its former reporters and a community board started The Owen News, a nonprofit that publishes twice a month. Its Oct. 16 edition reads like a county paying attention to itself: a state marker for Walden Cemetery, where Owen County's Black pioneers are buried; the EPA's update on groundwater contamination; a $25 million plan for a new campground at the state park; a community center in Cataract Lake that's struggling to get people in the door; and a big pot of beans that drew a big crowd. Four reporters' bylines in one issue, in a county of 21,000.

These are hand-dug wells. Each one started with someone who decided their town's lost journalism had to be replaced—by something. And the digging hasn't stopped.

Springs

Then there are the springs: people who came up on their own, often outside any institution, often with names that tell you exactly who they're for—a small Iowa town, Black business owners on the Georgia coast, school sports fans across southern Illinois. Here's an honest read of four: what makes each promising, and what makes it fragile.

The E'ville Good

Estherville, Iowa

Amy H. Peterson publishes in English and Spanish, and before an August heat wave she ran the headline "Here's what I know, what I don't, and where I need your help."

Peterson and McGill—along with the Neshobe Current's Carey Bunker—are News Creator Corps Rural Fellows, recruited from communities of 50,000 or fewer, no journalism experience required.

A caveat: most of the 'springs' we found sit near the driest counties, not inside them. In the southern Illinois counties that lost their papers this June, what we could find on TikTok and Facebook was football highlights, an unsigned livestream of a county board meeting and one resident filming a data-center fight. The desert map isn't wrong about where it's hardest. But counting mastheads inside county lines can hide the reporting that flows through those communities, carried by individuals, new institutions and new products.

Others Reading the Watershed

We're not the only ones looking at the map this way, and the people doing this work have sharpened our own. Chicago Public Media launched chicago.com this month on a finding from thousands of residents: people already assemble local information from outlets, newsletters, neighborhood groups and social media, and they want an easier way to find it and act on it. That's a watershed map, built as a product. Darryl Holliday and Anika Anand at Commoner Company, in This Is Local News Now for Press Forward, gave the rest of us the vocabulary: local news providers, civic promoters and community catalysts. Rosien and the Owen News are providers; Brandon's volunteers are catalysts who became providers. What journalism adds, they argue, is verification. Tom Rosenstiel's new book, The Next Journalism, starts news from people's questions—Peterson's heat post is that book in a newsletter. And City Bureau's Documenters show the digging can be taught and paid.

The Picks and Shovels

The hardest part is the money, and it pools where there's already water. By Medill's count, more than 95% of the largest journalism grants went to urban recipients, and nearly 80% of new local outlets launched in urban areas.

Look at what the people in this piece are working with. Rosien has a nonprofit and a name. Peterson has one foundation grant, from Joyce. The Neshobe Current's website is paid for by a volunteer, for one year. The Owen News is the lucky one: its county's community foundation put up a planning grant and an impact grant. The program that found many of the springs pays a $3,500 stipend for six weeks. Then what?

Here's one way to see what enough looks like. Four years in, the Sebastopol Times—a Substack in a California town of 7,500—has about 1,400 paying readers at $60 a year. Its co-publisher puts the whole operation at roughly $100,000: one reporter's salary and a small office. That's the price of one well that runs year-round, in a town with the money and the habit to pay for it. Most of the 238 counties have neither. None of what they have is a system. It's people, holding on.

We've built the system before. In The Creation of the Media, Paul Starr shows how the young country wired its hinterlands on purpose. The Post Office Act of 1792 carried newspapers at cut rates and pushed postal routes into places that could never pay for themselves; by 1831 the United States had about five times as many post offices per person as Britain. Getting news to thin places was treated as public infrastructure, not a market outcome. The same law let papers mail copies to each other for free, so a county-seat weekly could reprint news from the state capital and the capital could hear from the county seat.

Mapping the Basin

Elinor Ostrom started her career studying a groundwater basin that was going dry. In the 1940s, so many pumpers were drawing on West Basin, south of Los Angeles, that the water level kept falling and seawater crept in. No single water agency could fix it alone. So in 1946 they formed an association—and before they could agree on rules, they shared what they knew and studied the basin together. Over the next two decades they cut withdrawals by 30%, and the water came back. Ostrom built a Nobel Prize-winning body of work on that lesson: communities can govern a shared resource without handing it to a market or a single authority. Many centers, not one. But only if they can see it.

Local information is that kind of resource. It belongs to no single outlet, and it crosses county lines. Our problem runs the other way from West Basin—too few wells, not too many pumps—but the first step is the same. You can't tend a basin you haven't mapped. That goes for us too: until this month, the Atlas hadn't mapped anyone in the six southern Illinois counties that lost their papers in June. Medill mapped the institutions. It's time to map the wells, and to keep mapping them, because they change month to month.

It's why we map people at the Atlas, not just mastheads.
