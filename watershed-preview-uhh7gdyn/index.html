A Watershed Moment for News Deserts

Medill's map shows where local news has dried up. Look closer and it's still moving — across county lines, through people no count yet sees. Finding them takes a new kind of map.

By Justin Bank

I've never been to Harrisburg, Illinois. But from a laptop, you can learn a fair amount about Saline County. The sheriff posts. The weather service pushes heat advisories. The funeral home puts up the obituaries. What you can't learn is why — why the county board voted the way it did, what the school budget means, whether anyone is even asking. Harrisburg's paper, the Daily Register, printed its last edition in June, one of at least 13 Illinois papers Paddock Publications closed. Medill's researchers went in September, and for what's on the ground there, I'm relying on them. They found an April edition still sitting in the box outside the office.

Medill's State of Local News report counts 238 counties like Saline, with "no locally based source of news" — 26 more than a year ago, and five of them in southern Illinois after Paddock's closures alone. That count should worry anyone who cares about self-government. It worries me.

This year Medill also looked at independent creators, and to find them its researchers turned to the Independent Journalism Atlas — the roughly 1,500 creator-journalists we'd mapped as of May. They found that "less than 10% of this sample is covering local issues about their community." Our data from May says the same thing. They're right that these people are hard to find. The platforms don't make it easy.

We kept mapping. The Atlas now holds more than 2,400 creator-journalists, and the closer we look, the blurrier the categories get. In June, with Press Forward Chicago, we built an open toolkit on how creator-journalists and philanthropy can work together, and a City Lab prototype that mapped 39 independent journalists on local beats like city hall and schools. Thirteen of them already appear in Medill's own lists, counted as outlets. We've since baked City Lab into the main Atlas and into how we think about every place we map. Today the Atlas counts 111 local creator-journalists in Chicago.

As my co-founder Ryan Kellett puts it, it's journalism, not journalists. We had this argument once before, about bloggers, and some of those bloggers became the journalists we celebrate now.

I've been an editor at the Washington Post, the New York Times and NPR, and I watched the platforms cut the pipes that carried news, national and local. Penny Abernathy put "news desert" on the industry's map with her landmark work at UNC and made the loss impossible to ignore. But "desert" is the wrong word for what's left. A desert is empty. These places aren't.

Look at the ground and news moves like water. It doesn't stop at a county line. It runs off the surface, sinks underground and comes up wherever someone digs. In these counties there's plenty on the surface — the sheriff's posts, the alerts, the Facebook pages, the radio station one county over and, more and more, automated sites dressed up as local news, posting Saline County's weekly gas prices with nobody there. Most of it runs off. It tells you what happened and almost never why.

Fifteen years ago, Steve Waldman's report for the FCC warned that this kind of abundance is an "illusion of bounty" masking a shortage of reporting. These places don't lack information. They have information without journalism. What's scarce is the digging — someone who goes to the meeting, reads the minutes and explains what the vote means for the people who live there.

People feel the difference. Medill's own survey found that people in news deserts trust the news media far less than people in places well served by local news: 46% to 59%.

The people who do dig are hard to see. Counts see mastheads, money follows counts, and the people doing the work in the thinnest places show up in neither.

But in more places than the desert map shows, someone has picked up a shovel. This piece is about them. And we'll be using a visual library of four terms to bring a bit of nuance to the Watersheds we see emerging around these news deserts:

Wells are newsrooms that neighbors rebuild after a paper dies. They have a masthead, an address and a history.

Springs are people with no paper behind them — a page, a feed, a channel, a stream. Some come up on their own. Some get seeded.

Flow is coverage that crosses a county line.

Surface is information that runs off: the alerts and posts that tell you what happened and almost never why.

Hand-Dug Wells

Wells pick up where a paper left off. Most are dug by people who were in the room when the paper died, or watched it hollow out — the editor, the publisher, the reader who showed up to the last-ditch meeting. Their tools are modest: a nonprofit filing, a newsletter platform, a handful of volunteers.

In Anaconda, Montana, the Leader printed its final paper on June 5. Days later the town had its second fatal public shooting in a year. James Rosien, who had edited the paper since 2011, heard the sirens, got in his car and followed them, then asked himself why: he had no platform left to report it. "It feels like the heart and soul of the community just got scattered," he told Montana Public Radio. He and his neighbors named their new paper the Anaconda Weekly Review, after the town's first newspaper from the 1880s, and began publishing this August. Deer Lodge County is one of Medill's 238 in the 2026 count, even though the Review was printing by August. It has a newspaper again.

In Tarboro, North Carolina, John Walker ran the Daily Southerner, and now he runs My Tarboro Today. He still goes to the meetings and takes notes for his neighbors on a plain website he keeps up himself. Edgecombe County is also one of the 238.

In Brandon, Vermont, look at one week of the Neshobe Current: a selectboard story on surprising sludge costs and paying off the police station bond; who's funding the local state rep races; the WWII dead of Brandon High School. "We're more of a community newsletter than a source of original news," volunteer Jeff Weston told Vermont Public.

I think Weston is being modest. Six neighbors working out a sludge bill and explaining it is the work of journalism.

Springs

Wells are easier to spot once you start looking. They have names and mastheads. Springs are harder. They're narrower than a general-interest paper: a source of news and information that may cover one board, one team, one town's schools. And the springs we found move with the currents of the platforms.

Inside the five southern Illinois counties that lost their papers in June, the springs don't look like newsrooms. In Harrisburg, Bulldog Media streams the high school's games to Facebook and YouTube for families who can't make the drive. It sits the head coach down before every game and sells spots to the pharmacy and the eye doctor in town. It has posted more than 300 videos. In Goreville, in Johnson County, someone with the old school yearbooks runs Goreville History, posting a page at a time while former classmates fill the comments with names. In McLeansboro, in Hamilton County, a woman who runs a market in town films what's happening on Main Street, like the house everyone decorates for Halloween. In Old Shawneetown, in Gallatin County, a local artist tells the history of the floods and levees that shaped her town. In Pulaski County, we haven't found one yet.

In Gallatin County, an unsigned page called Gallatin County Board (Unofficial) livestreams the board's meetings and posts its agendas on Facebook and YouTube. Each stream gets a few shares. Questions in the comments mostly go unanswered. Nobody explains what happened. But someone decided the meetings should be seen.

None of them would call it journalism, and most of it isn't, at least not yet. Together, it's how a county keeps track of itself: neighbors using tools built far away to make something that works here.

Farther out, some springs got help. In Estherville, Iowa, Amy H. Peterson publishes The E'ville Good about once a month, in English and Spanish. Before an August heat wave she ran the headline "Here's what I know, what I don't, and where I need your help." On the Georgia coast, Courtney McGill's Black-Owned Brunswick covers businesses, creatives and culture for people who rarely got coverage of their own. Both are News Creator Corps Rural Fellows — recruited from communities of 50,000 or fewer, no journalism experience required, and paid to get started.

Earlier this year we went looking for what's working for local news on Substack. One of the best cases we found was the Sebastopol Times, named for the print paper that once served a Sonoma County town of 7,500. Four years in, it had about 1,400 paying subscribers out of 5,700 readers. That's a 24.5% conversion rate; national news Substacks typically convert about 3%. Proximity converts. But the money clustered where it always has — in travel, sports and towns with the income and the habit to pay for news.

So when we scanned the desert counties, we looked for the next Sebastopol. We didn't find one — not in Saline, not in its neighbors, not in the roughly 40 desert counties we checked nationally. The subscription newsletter, as a platform, a revenue mix and a product, doesn't seem to translate to places without that money and that habit. Not yet, anyway.

What does show up runs on the open web and on vertical video: a board meeting streamed to Facebook and YouTube, a radio station's website, sports channels and TikTok accounts that gather a region's news into a feed. It's the poly-platform path we've been tracking in creator journalism, showing up where it matters most. Desert springs don't live on Substack, or at least we haven't found any yet. They live in streams and feeds.

Confluence

In southern Illinois, the digging mostly starts next door, and the news flows in. In Carmi, WRUL, a small-town radio station in White County, has covered the Hamilton County board next door since at least 2019. The ABC station that serves the region, WSIL, is still licensed to Harrisburg; it moved its studios to Carterville in 1989. Sports is often the doorway. Ballin4God, a channel covering southern Illinois high school sports, followed Friday nights into the building: on Oct. 18 it interviewed Harrisburg's principal. The next day, two TikTok accounts from the Carbondale and Marion area, Aaron Price and @soillsportswithchrisdex, posted about the schools in Harrisburg. It's tempting to wave off a sports page. In a county without a paper, the account that's there every Friday night may be the one people already count on to show up.

Some of it crosses a state line. When Johnson County's vote on its shuttered, 155-year-old courthouse needed explaining this month — two competing price tags, who pays, what happens if nobody does — the explaining came from WPSD, a TV station in Paducah, Kentucky. When Pulaski County's sheriff's office made the news, it was from Cape Girardeau, Missouri.

Nobody counts any of these as outlets. We haven't talked to the people behind these pages and accounts yet, so we can't say who they are or whether they'd want to be counted. And a feed isn't an archive. If the app goes, the record goes with it.

Illinois law already knows how the water moves. When a town has no paper of its own, the state lets its legal notices run in any newspaper that circulates there — in these counties, one from over the line. The law assumes the news can come from somewhere else. It just assumes it comes in a newspaper.

No single source in Anaconda does what the Leader did. The new weekly covers the county; Copperhead Country covers Friday nights; Montana Public Radio told the rest of the state when the Leader closed. Together they're a start, and only one of them would show up on a map of mastheads.

Some people build the confluence on purpose. In the Upper Valley of Vermont and New Hampshire, Rob Gurwitt started Daybreak in 2019 with 25 subscribers. Today more than 15,000 people in about 42 towns open it every weekday, mostly to find everyone else's reporting in one place. It's only as strong as the wells around it.

Others are drawing the same map. Darryl Holliday and Anika Anand at Commoner Company gave us the vocabulary in This Is Local News Now, their report for Press Forward: local news providers, civic promoters and community catalysts, with journalism adding verification. Tom Rosenstiel's new book, The Next Journalism, starts news from people's questions — Peterson's heat post is that idea in a newsletter. And Chicago Public Media just launched chicago.com on what thousands of residents told them: they already piece local information together from outlets, newsletters, neighborhood groups and social media, and they want an easier way to find it. That's a watershed map, built as a product.

Picks and Shovels

Picks and shovels are the money and tools that keep the digging going. The hardest part is the money, and it pools where there's already water. By Medill's count, more than 95% of the largest journalism grants went to urban recipients, and nearly 80% of new local outlets launched in urban areas.

Look at what the people in this piece are working with. Rosien has a nonprofit and a name. The Neshobe Current's website is paid for by one volunteer, for one year. In Owen County, Indiana, The Owen News got lucky: its county's community foundation put up a planning grant and an impact grant. The program that recruited Peterson and McGill pays a $3,500 stipend for six weeks. A well that runs year-round, like the Sebastopol Times, takes about $100,000 a year — and a town that can pay for it. The gap has a price, too. After a local paper closes, the local government's borrowing costs go up. Without someone watching, lenders charge more.

This isn't the first time thin places needed help getting the news. In The Creation of the Media, Paul Starr shows how the young country wired its hinterlands on purpose. The Post Office Act of 1792 carried newspapers at cut rates and pushed postal routes into places that could never pay for themselves. Getting news to thin places was treated as public infrastructure, not a market outcome.

Illinois built a modern version. In 2024 it became the first state to pay local newsrooms directly to keep and hire journalists, through refundable tax credits, and the early results are encouraging. Then it lost more newspapers than any other state, Medill found. The credit runs through payroll: to claim it, you need at least one full-time journalist on staff. Whoever keeps the Gallatin County board livestream running can't claim it. Neither can Bulldog Media, or the accounts covering Harrisburg's schools from next door.

Part of the answer already works in cities. City Bureau's Documenters program pays residents to sit through public meetings and write down what happened. The digging can be taught, and it can be paid for.

Mapping the Basin

Elinor Ostrom started her career studying a groundwater basin that was going dry. In the 1940s, so many pumpers were drawing on West Basin, south of Los Angeles, that seawater crept in. No single agency could fix it. By 1946 they had formed an association, and before they agreed on rules, they studied the basin together. Ostrom built a Nobel Prize-winning body of work on that lesson: communities can govern a shared resource without handing it to a market or a single authority. But only if they can see it.

Local information is that kind of resource. It belongs to no single outlet, and it crosses county lines. Our problem runs the other way from West Basin: we have too few wells, not too many pumps. But the first step is the same. You can't tend a basin you haven't mapped.

That goes for us, too. Until this month, the Atlas hadn't mapped a single person in the five southern Illinois counties that lost their papers in June. Medill mapped the institutions. Now someone has to map the people, and keep mapping them, because they change. And they'll change faster than newspapers turned into websites. Whatever comes next, in a world of AI-powered products and a fractured attention economy, will run on the trust people place in the humans who find information and pass it on.

We can't do that alone. Press Forward's local chapters, libraries and state press associations already know who's digging in their places. The Urban Libraries Council is pairing public libraries with local newsrooms, and Terry Parris Jr.'s Library Newsroom Project is building local information practices inside public libraries.

That's why the Atlas maps people, not just mastheads, and why mapping, for us, means checking: who they are, whether they show up, whether the place relies on them. If you know who your county trusts to tell it what's going on — a creator, an outlet or the neighbor who goes to every meeting — tell us (for this preview, just reply to the note that brought you here). And if you fund local news, let us show you who's already there.

Back in Harrisburg, the sheriff will post again tomorrow. The alert will land, and run off. The question this watershed moment asks is whether we back the people already digging, or keep drawing deserts around them.

There's so much more to find.
