A Watershed Moment for News Deserts

Medill's map shows where local news has dried up. Look closer and it's still moving: across county lines, through people no count yet sees. Finding them takes a new kind of map.

By Justin Bank

I've never been to Harrisburg, Illinois. But from a laptop, you can still learn a fair amount about Saline County. The sheriff posts. The weather service pushes heat advisories. The funeral home puts up the obituaries. What's harder to learn is why: why the county board voted the way it did, what the school budget means, or even whether anyone is asking. Harrisburg's paper, the Daily Register, printed its last edition in June, one of at least 13 Illinois papers Paddock Publications closed. Medill's researchers went in September, and for what's on the ground there, I'm relying on them. They found an April edition still sitting in the box outside the office.

Medill's State of Local News report counts 238 counties like Saline, with "no locally based source of news" — 26 more than a year ago, and five of them in southern Illinois after Paddock's closures alone. That count should worry anyone who cares about self-government. It worries me.

This year's report also looked at independent creators, and to find them, Medill's researchers turned to the Independent Journalism Atlas, drawing on the roughly 1,500 creator-journalists we'd mapped as of May. They found that "less than 10% of this sample is covering local issues about their community." Our data from that moment says the same thing, and they're right that finding these people is hard. The platforms don't make it easy.

Since May, the Atlas has grown to more than 2,400 creator-journalists. In June we happened to go deep on Chicago as part of our work with Press Forward Chicago, building an open case study on how creator journalists can work with philanthropists. We ended up finding more creators, and that's where the categories started to blur: of the 43 independent journalists we've mapped there on local beats like city hall and schools, 13 are already on Medill's list, counted as local outlets.

I've worked as an editor at the Washington Post, the New York Times and NPR, and lived through the years when the pipes that carried news got cut by Big Tech, national and local. Penny Abernathy put "news desert" on the industry's map with her landmark work at UNC, and made the loss impossible to ignore. But "desert" is the wrong word for what's left. A desert is empty. These places are not.

Look at the ground and news moves like water. It doesn't stop at a county line. It runs off the surface, sinks underground and comes up wherever someone digs. In these counties it's plentiful on the surface: the sheriff's posts, the alerts, the Facebook pages, the radio station one county over and, more and more, automated sites dressed as local news, posting about the county with no one there. Most of it runs off. It tells you what happened and almost never why.

Fifteen years ago, Steve Waldman's report for the FCC warned that this kind of abundance is an "illusion of bounty" masking a shortage of reporting. These places don't lack information. They have information without journalism. What's scarce is the digging: someone who goes to the meeting, reads the minutes and reports on how government interacts with daily local life.

These places don't lack information. They have information without journalism.

But in more places than the desert map shows, there are people working to organize and report on news in their local areas. This piece is about those people, and it uses three terms to navigate across these different scenes.

Wells are newsrooms neighbors rebuild after a paper dies; they have a masthead, an address and a history.

Springs are people who come up on their own with no paper behind them: a page, a feed, a channel, a newsletter.

Picks and shovels are the money and tools that keep the digging going.

Hand-Dug Wells

Wells pick up where a paper left off. Many are being dug by folks who were in the room when their local paper died, or watched it hollow out: the editor, the publisher, the reader who showed up to the last-ditch meeting. Their tools are modest — a nonprofit filing, a newsletter platform, a handful of volunteers.

In Anaconda, Montana, the Leader printed its final paper on June 5. Days later the town had its second fatal public shooting in a year. "It feels like the heart and soul of the community just got scattered," James Rosien, who had edited the paper since 2011, told Montana Public Radio. He and his neighbors named their new paper the Anaconda Weekly Review, after the town's first newspaper from the 1880s, and began publishing this August. Deer Lodge County is one of Medill's 238. It has a newspaper again.

In Tarboro, North Carolina, John Walker ran the Daily Southerner, and now he runs My Tarboro Today. He still goes to the meetings and takes notes for his neighbors. Edgecombe County is also one of the 238.

In Brandon, Vermont, look at one week of the Neshobe Current: a selectboard story on surprising sludge costs and paying off the police station bond; who's funding the local state rep races; the WWII dead of Brandon High School. "We're more of a community newsletter than a source of original news," volunteer Jeff Weston told Vermont Public. Weston is being modest. Six neighbors explaining a sludge bill is the work.

Springs

The wells are easy to see once you look; they have names and mastheads. The springs are harder, and tend to carry more purely distilled information than the general-purpose bundles we associate with 20th-century newspapers.

Inside the five southern Illinois counties that lost their papers in June, we found a few of these springs.

In Gallatin County, next door to Saline, an unsigned page called Gallatin County Board (Unofficial) livestreams the board's meetings and posts its agendas on Facebook and YouTube, and keeps doing it. Nobody explains what happened. But someone decided the meetings should be seen.

Sports is often the doorway. Ballin4God, a channel covering southern Illinois high school sports, followed Friday nights into the building: on Oct. 18 it interviewed Harrisburg's principal.

Farther out, some springs have grown steadier. In Estherville, Iowa, Amy H. Peterson publishes The E'ville Good about once a month, in English and Spanish; before an August heat wave she ran the headline "Here's what I know, what I don't, and where I need your help." On the Georgia coast, Courtney McGill's Black-Owned Brunswick covers businesses, creatives and culture for people who rarely got coverage of their own. Both are News Creator Corps Rural Fellows, recruited from communities of 50,000 or fewer, no journalism experience required.

The desert map isn't wrong about where it's hardest. But counting mastheads inside county lines hides the reporting that flows through those places, carried by individuals, new institutions and new products.

Confluence

In southern Illinois, the digging mostly starts next door, and the news flows in. In Carmi, WRUL, a small-town radio station in White County, has covered the Hamilton County board next door since at least 2019. On Oct. 19, two TikTok accounts from the Carbondale and Marion area, Aaron Price and @soillsportswithchrisdex, posted about the schools in Harrisburg. Nobody counts these accounts as outlets. We haven't talked to the people behind them yet, so we can't say who they are or whether they'd want to be counted. And a feed isn't an archive.

Illinois law already knows how the water moves. When a town has no newspaper of its own, the state lets its legal notices run in any newspaper that circulates there, which in these counties means one from over the line. The law assumes the news can come from somewhere else. It just assumes it comes in a newspaper.

No single source in Anaconda does what the Leader did. The new weekly covers the county; Copperhead Country covers Friday nights; Montana Public Radio told the rest of the state when the Leader closed. Together they're a start, and only one of them would show up on a map of mastheads.

Some people build the confluence on purpose. In the Upper Valley of Vermont and New Hampshire, Rob Gurwitt started Daybreak in 2019 with 25 subscribers. Now more than 15,000 people in about 42 towns open it every weekday, mostly to find everyone else's reporting in one place. It's only as strong as the wells around it. That's the point.

We're not the only ones looking at the map this way. Chicago Public Media launched chicago.com this month on a finding from thousands of residents: people already assemble local information from outlets, newsletters, neighborhood groups and social media, and they want an easier way to find it. That's a watershed map, built as a product. Darryl Holliday and Anika Anand at Commoner Company, in This Is Local News Now for Press Forward, gave the rest of us the vocabulary: local news providers, civic promoters and community catalysts. What journalism adds, they argue, is verification. And Tom Rosenstiel's new book, The Next Journalism, starts news from people's questions. Peterson's heat post is that book in a newsletter.

Picks and Shovels

The hardest part is the money, and it pools where there's already water. By Medill's count, more than 95% of the largest journalism grants went to urban recipients, and nearly 80% of new local outlets launched in urban areas.

Look at what the people in this piece are working with. Rosien has a nonprofit and a name. The Neshobe Current's website is paid for by a volunteer, for one year. In Owen County, Indiana, The Owen News got lucky: its county's community foundation put up a planning grant and an impact grant. The program that found many of the springs pays a $3,500 stipend for six weeks. Then what? A well that runs year-round, like the Sebastopol Times, costs about $100,000 a year, in a town with the money and the habit to pay for it. And the gap has its own price: after a local paper closes, the government's borrowing costs go up. Without someone watching, lenders charge more.

Part of the answer is already working in cities. City Bureau's Documenters pay residents to sit through public meetings and write down what happened. The digging can be taught, and paid.

When we looked at what's working for local news on Substack, the money was in travel, sports and wealthier towns. We found almost nothing from places like Harrisburg, which is why this search went to TikTok, YouTube, Facebook pages and a radio station's website instead. What most of the 238 counties have isn't a system. It's people, holding on.

This isn't the first time thin places needed help to get the news. In The Creation of the Media, Paul Starr shows how the young country wired its hinterlands on purpose: the Post Office Act of 1792 carried newspapers at cut rates and pushed postal routes into places that could never pay for themselves. Getting news to thin places was treated as public infrastructure, not a market outcome.

Illinois built a modern version. In 2024 it became the first state to pay local newsrooms directly to keep and hire journalists, through refundable tax credits, and the early results are encouraging. Then, by Medill's count, it lost more newspapers than any other state. The credit runs through payroll: to claim it, you need at least one full-time journalist on staff. Whoever keeps the Gallatin County board livestream running can't. Neither can the accounts covering Harrisburg's schools from next door.

Mapping the Basin

Elinor Ostrom started her career studying a groundwater basin that was going dry. In the 1940s, so many pumpers were drawing on West Basin, south of Los Angeles, that seawater crept in. No single agency could fix it. By 1946 they had formed an association, and before they agreed on rules, they studied the basin together. Ostrom built a Nobel Prize-winning body of work on that lesson: communities can govern a shared resource without handing it to a market or a single authority. But only if they can see it.

Local information is that kind of resource. It belongs to no single outlet, and it crosses county lines. Our problem runs the other way from West Basin — too few wells, not too many pumps — but the first step is the same. You can't tend a basin you haven't mapped. That goes for us too: until this month, the Atlas hadn't mapped anyone in the five southern Illinois counties that lost their papers in June. Medill mapped the institutions. It's time to map the people, and to keep mapping them, because they change month to month. We can't do that alone. Press Forward's local chapters, libraries and state press associations already know who's digging in their places.

You can't tend a basin you haven't mapped.

That's why the Atlas maps people, not just mastheads, and why mapping, for us, means checking: who they are, whether they show up, whether the place relies on them. Back in Harrisburg, the sheriff will post again tomorrow. The alert will land, and run off. The question this watershed moment asks is whether we back the people already digging, or keep drawing deserts around them.
