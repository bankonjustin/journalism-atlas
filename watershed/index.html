A Watershed Moment for News Deserts

Medill's map shows where local news has dried up. Look closer and information is still moving — across county lines, and through platforms, beats and forms the count doesn't see.

By Justin Bank

I've never been to Harrisburg, Illinois. But from a laptop, you can learn a fair amount about Saline County. The sheriff posts. The weather service pushes heat advisories. The funeral home puts up the obituaries. What you can't learn is why — why the county board voted the way it did, what the school budget means, whether anyone is even asking. Harrisburg's paper, the Daily Register, printed its last edition in June, one of at least 13 Illinois papers Paddock Publications closed.

Researchers from Northwestern's Medill School of Journalism did visit, in September. Among other things, they found an April edition still sitting in the box outside the office.

Medill's State of Local News report counts 238 counties like Saline, with "no locally based source of professional local news" — 26 more than a year ago, and five of them in southern Illinois after Paddock's closures alone. That count should worry anyone who cares about self-government. It worries me.

This year, for the first time, Medill also looked at independent creators, and to find them its researchers turned to the Independent Journalism Atlas — the roughly 1,500 creator-journalists we'd mapped as of May. They found that "less than 10% of this sample is covering local issues about their community." Our data from May says the same thing, and so does a Montclair State study of New Jersey influencers this summer: about one post in nine was core local news. They're right that these people are hard to find. The platforms don't make it easy.

Penny Abernathy didn't coin "news desert," but her landmark work at UNC made it the industry's word and made the loss impossible to ignore. She picked it on purpose. As she told Medill: "I've always liked the term 'desert' because things grow in a desert." Most people hear something else: a desert is empty. These places aren't.

Look at the ground and news moves like water. It doesn't stop at a county line. It runs off the surface, sinks underground and comes up wherever someone digs. In these counties there's plenty on the surface — the sheriff's posts, the alerts, the Facebook pages, the radio station one county over and, more and more, automated sites dressed up as local news, posting Saline County's weekly gas prices with nobody there. Most of it runs off. It tells you what happened and almost never why.

Fifteen years ago, Steve Waldman's report for the FCC found that "breathtaking media abundance lives side-by-side with serious shortages in reporting." These places don't lack information. They have information without journalism. What's scarce is the digging — someone who goes to the meeting, reads the minutes and explains what the vote means for the people who live there.

People feel the difference. Medill's own survey found that people in news deserts trust the news media far less than people in places well served by local news: 46% to 59%.

The people doing the digging are hard to see. Most counts, by trade groups and academics alike, look for mastheads. Money follows the counts, and the people doing the work in the thinnest places show up in neither.

But in more places than the desert map shows, someone has picked up a shovel. This piece is about them. Every community and every contributor is different, but look across enough of them and patterns emerge:

Hand-Dug Wells

Wells pick up where a paper left off. Most are dug by people who were in the building when the paper died, or watched it hollow out — the editor, the publisher, the reader who showed up to the last-ditch meeting. Their tools are often modest: a nonprofit filing, a newsletter platform, a handful of volunteers.

Sometimes institutions dig, too. This fall, Capitol News Illinois and the Illinois Press Foundation, with SIU's journalism school, started Southern Illinois Source, a nonprofit newsroom for the region whose stories any outlet can republish.

In Anaconda, Montana, the Leader printed its final paper on June 5. Days later the town had its second fatal public shooting in a year. James Rosien, who had worked at the Leader since 2011 and was its editor-in-chief, heard the sirens, got in his car and followed them, then asked himself why: he had no platform left to report it. "To me, it feels like the heart and soul of the community just got scattered after the closure," he told Montana Public Radio. He and his neighbors started the Anaconda Weekly Review, which began publishing this August. Deer Lodge County is still one of Medill's 238. It has a newspaper again.

In Brandon, Vermont, look at a few days of the Neshobe Current: a selectboard story on surprising sludge costs and paying off the police station bond; who's funding the local state rep races; the WWII dead of Brandon High School.

"We're more of a community newsletter than a source of original news," volunteer Jeff Weston told Vermont Public.

I think Weston is being modest. Six neighbors working out a sludge bill and explaining it is the work of journalism.

Springs

Springs are harder to spot than wells. They're narrower than a paper — one board, one team, one town's schools. And they move with the currents of the platforms.

Inside the five southern Illinois counties that lost their papers in June, the clearest spring we've found is a camera. In Gallatin County, next door to Saline, an unsigned page called Gallatin County Board (Unofficial) livestreams the board's meetings and posts its agendas on Facebook and YouTube, and keeps doing it. Nobody explains what happened. But someone decided the meetings should be seen.

Farther out, some springs got help. In Estherville, Iowa, Amy H. Peterson publishes The E'ville Good in English and Spanish. Before an August heat wave she ran the headline "Here's what I know, what I don't, and where I need your help." On the Georgia coast, Courtney McGill's Black-Owned Brunswick covers businesses, creatives and culture for people who rarely got coverage of their own. Both are News Creator Corps Rural Fellows — recruited from rural communities and paid to get started.

Earlier this year we went looking for what works for local news on Substack. One of the best cases was the Sebastopol Times, in a Sonoma County town of 7,500, where about a quarter of readers pay. Proximity converts. But the money clustered where it always has, in towns with the income and the habit to pay for news.

After Medill's report, we looked for the next Sebastopol in the desert counties: in Saline, in its neighbors and in 40 others we swept nationally. We didn't find one. Not yet, anyway.

What does show up in these regions tends to run on the open web and on vertical video: a board meeting streamed to Facebook and YouTube, a radio station's website, sports channels and TikTok accounts that gather a region's news into a feed. It's the poly-platform path we've been tracking in creator journalism, showing up where it matters most. Desert springs don't live on Substack, or at least we haven't found any yet. They live in streams and feeds.

Confluence

When enough springs and wells run together, you get a confluence. In southern Illinois, much of the digging happens next door, and the news flows in.

In Carmi, WRUL, a small-town radio station in White County, has covered the Hamilton County board next door since at least 2019. Sports is often the doorway. Ballin4God, a channel covering southern Illinois high school sports, followed Friday nights into the building: on Oct. 18 it interviewed Harrisburg's principal. The next day, two TikTok accounts from the Carbondale and Marion area, Aaron Price and @soillsportswithchrisdex, posted about the schools in Harrisburg.

It's tempting to wave off a sports page. But prep sports was the most consistent pattern we saw across the desert counties we researched: Facebook pages streaming games, TikTok accounts interviewing coaches, sites tracking stats. In a county without a paper, the account that's there every Friday night may be the one people already count on to show up. We haven't talked to the people behind these accounts yet, so we can't say who they are, individually or as a cohort, or whether they'd call what they do journalism.

Whatever they call it, what they're recording is a public good, and it's worth more if it lasts. A feed isn't an archive. If the app goes, the record goes with it. Much of it is already out of reach: in none of the 24 counties we swept could we fully read the loudest local source without signing in.

The rules haven't caught up, either. Illinois law already knows news can come from the county next door: when a town has no paper of its own, the state lets its legal notices run in any newspaper that circulates there — in these counties, one from over the line. It just assumes the news comes in a newspaper. In May, Hamilton County's notices said "Publish: McLeansboro Gazette." The Gazette has since closed, and October's notices name no paper at all.

Back in Anaconda, no single source does what the Leader did. The new weekly covers the county; Copperhead Country covers Friday nights; Montana Public Radio told the rest of the state when the Leader closed. Together they're a start, and only one of them would show up on a map of mastheads.

Some people build the confluence on purpose. In the Upper Valley of Vermont and New Hampshire, Rob Gurwitt started Daybreak in 2019 with 25 subscribers. Today more than 15,000 people in about 42 towns get it every weekday, mostly to find everyone else's reporting in one place. It's only as strong as the wells around it.

Picks and Shovels

Picks and shovels are the money and tools that keep the digging going, and the money pools where there's already water. By Medill's count, more than 95% of the largest journalism grants went to urban recipients, and nearly 80% of new local outlets launched in urban areas. When we checked 12 funding programs open to local news, one funded a solo creator directly, and five required a sponsor or partner.

Look at what the people in this piece are working with. Rosien has a nonprofit and a name. The Neshobe Current's website is paid for by one volunteer, for one year. In Owen County, Indiana, The Owen News got lucky: its county's community foundation put up a planning grant and an impact grant. The gap has a price, too. After a local paper closes, the local government's borrowing costs go up. The authors tie it to lost oversight: without someone watching, lenders charge more.

This isn't the first time thin places needed help getting the news. In The Creation of the Media, Paul Starr shows how the Post Office Act of 1792 carried newspapers at cut rates and pushed postal routes into places that could never pay for themselves. Getting news to thin places was public infrastructure, not a market outcome.

Illinois built a modern version. In 2024 it passed a law making it the first state to pay local newsrooms directly to keep and hire journalists, through refundable tax credits, and the early results are encouraging. Then it lost more newspapers than any other state, Medill found. The credit runs through payroll: to claim it, you need at least one full-time journalist on staff. Whoever keeps the Gallatin County board livestream running can't claim it. Neither can the accounts covering Harrisburg's schools from next door.

Part of the answer already works in cities. City Bureau's Documenters program pays residents to sit through public meetings and write down what happened. The digging can be taught, and it can be paid for.

Mapping the Basin

Elinor Ostrom started her career studying a groundwater basin that was being pumped faster than it refilled. By the 1940s, so many pumpers were drawing on West Basin, south of Los Angeles, that water levels had dropped below sea level and seawater had begun to creep in. No single agency could fix it. A survey committee started studying the basin in the early 1940s. By 1946 the pumpers had formed an association, and it took until 1961 for most of the parties to sign a settlement that cut pumping. Ostrom built a Nobel Prize-winning body of work on that lesson: communities can govern a shared resource without handing it to a market or a single authority. But only if they can see it. Watching is one of her design principles for commons that last: whoever monitors the resource has to answer to the people who depend on it.

Local information is that kind of resource. It belongs to no single outlet, and it crosses county lines. Our problem runs the other way from West Basin: we have too few wells, not too many pumps. But the first step is the same. You can't tend a basin you haven't mapped.

That goes for us, too. Until this month, the Atlas hadn't mapped a single person in the five southern Illinois counties that lost their papers in June.

And yet the people in those counties are looking for news, and finding it in these new forms. Those forms will keep changing. Today's Substacks and prep-sports TikToks will evolve, probably faster than newspapers turned into websites. Whatever comes next, in a world of AI-powered products and a fractured attention economy, will run on the trust people place in the humans who find information and pass it on.

Charting the Watershed

We've kept mapping since May. The Atlas now holds more than 2,400 creator-journalists, and the closer we look, the blurrier the categories get.

This spring, with Press Forward Chicago, we built an open toolkit for Chicago's creator-journalists, on contracts, rates, revenue and grants, and a City Lab prototype that mapped 39 independent journalists on local beats like city hall and schools. Thirteen of them already appear in Medill's own lists, counted as outlets. Today the Atlas counts 111 local independent journalists in Chicago.

As my co-founder Ryan Kellett puts it, it's journalism, not journalists. Ryan spent his Nieman-Berkman fellowship studying creators who resist journalism's labels. Our other co-founder, Liz Kelly Nelson, built Project C, a community of creator-journalists, during her 2024 Sulzberger Fellowship at Columbia. As Liz put it at Newsgeist last year, humans are the atomic units of trust and journalism. As an elder millennial who came up in newsrooms in the era of the blogosphere and platform traffic waves, I can't believe we're having this argument about credentialing again. Plenty of those "new media" bloggers became the journalists we celebrate now.

That idea is what the Atlas is built on: we decide who belongs by the work, not by the title or the platform. Our standards come down to five tests. Ours is only one way of curating, and we admire and welcome others, from the humble blogrolls independent journalists keep to the in-depth reports of researchers like those at Northwestern.

What we look for

  1. Independent and identifiable: they own their channel, and you can tell who they are.
  2. A clear journalism product: a publication, channel or show, not personal branding.
  3. Sustained, recent effort: consistency over volume.
  4. A journalistic function: reporting, analysis, opinion, curation or education that serves what a community needs to know.
  5. Credibility and transparency: consistent quality, audience trust, disclosed leanings; fabrication, plagiarism or deceiving the audience rules someone out.

A good map draws on more than one vantage point. As we've charted geographies and beats that are new to us, we've leaned on Press Forward's local chapters, libraries, state press associations and many other journalism groups, formal and informal. They already know who's digging in their places.

For most of the last century, local news ran on moats: a masthead, a press, a delivery route nobody else could match. In a watershed, bridges are the new moats. The value is in the connection: the radio station that covers the next county's board, the livestream someone takes the time to explain, the creator a funder would never have found. The bridge builders are already at work, and a few we believe in are below. We'll keep charting and writing about what we find in the Atlas.

There is so much more to chart and build, in our communities, across and beyond platforms.

Builders and mappers we believe in

Mapping local information

  • chicago.com: Chicago Public Media built it on what thousands of residents said, that they already piece local information together and want an easier way to find it. A watershed map, built as a product.
  • News Creator Corps: seeds springs in towns of 50,000 or fewer, no journalism experience required.
  • Commoner Company: Darryl Holliday and Anika Anand's This Is Local News Now, for Press Forward, names local news providers, civic promoters and community catalysts.
  • The Next Journalism: Tom Rosenstiel starts news from people's questions; Peterson's heat post is that idea in a newsletter.
  • Urban Libraries Council: pairing public libraries with local newsrooms.
  • Library Newsroom Project: Terry Parris Jr. is building local information practices inside public libraries.
  • Local News Initiative: Medill keeps the count that started this conversation.

Building the ground it runs on

  • Holdfast: Erin Kissane, co-founder of the COVID Tracking Project, on infrastructure that anchors communities the way kelp holdfasts shelter life in rough water.
  • The Resonant Computing Manifesto: five principles for technology that serves people instead of extracting from them: private, dedicated to the user, plural and distributed, adaptable, prosocial.
  • Relational Tech Project: Josh Nesbit and Deborah Tien on tools built with and for neighbors, to foster care and connection.
