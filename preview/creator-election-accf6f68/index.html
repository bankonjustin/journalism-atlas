The Creator Election Reached Local TV. Its Youngest Reporters Brought It.

On election week, local TV's newest reporters covered how the vote happened, on their own feeds. Their stations covered the count, and mostly carried their reporters' work along with it. Nobody has written down whose it is.

By Justin Bank · Working draft 2, Nov. 14, 2026

The day before Louisiana chose a U.S. senator, a young reporter at FOX 8 in New Orleans followed the voting machines out the door under police escort, then told viewers what they needed to know: how the machines are secured, what's on the ballot, what the race is about. On Election Day she spent the day putting one question to voters, reported the people who politely passed as faithfully as the ones who answered, and set the answers against a national survey.

Watch the two posts back to back and you see a broadcast reporter's discipline, talking straight to the camera, carried in a creator's rhythm. The second post is a scene, not a stand-up.

Both posts went up on her own Instagram. We couldn't find either on FOX 8's accounts. The station's TikTok that week ran mostly network election content: national takeaways, the winner's speech, a generic what-to-know post. Its elections page listed 21 stories for Nov. 2 to 5, a solid run of races and results. None was about the machines or the voters' one question.

The creator election, inside the newsroom

The independent journalists the Atlas follows were everywhere this election. In election week they filed 3,442 pieces, and election talk in their headlines had more than tripled in three weeks, peaking in election week itself. Most of it was what independents do best: the national read, the predictions, the argument.

Less noticed: the creator economy's habits also turned up inside local broadcast newsrooms, carried by their youngest reporters. A personal feed that names the station. A voice of their own. Vertical video, often shot alone. Of 88 early-career on-air reporters the Atlas coded this fall, 76 post from personal accounts like that.

So we looked at election week through their feeds. We read more than 30 young reporters' feeds and the station accounts beside them, shortlisted 22 posts by 13 reporters in 12 markets, and watched the best of them. Then we lined up three feeds for Nov. 2 to 5: the young reporters, their stations' websites, and the independents.

Three feeds, three jobs

The young reporters covered the vote. Their stations covered the count. The independents covered what it meant.

Of the young reporters' 22 posts, 14 explained how voting or counting worked or showed what was happening on the ground. On their stations' websites, two of every three election stories were results. The independents, mostly on newsletters, spent the week on predictions, arguments and what the night meant for Congress, and that held even for independents in the same states as our reporters.

Take the numbers as a sketch. The reporters' posts are the best we found, not a sample, and we picked them partly for explaining well, which favors process. Station counts are a minimum, read mostly from headlines and capped at each station's newest ten stories, which tilts them toward the day after. Independents were sorted mostly by title. A second coder, working blind, agreed with the first 81% of the time, and on every process and place call.

The best of the week

  1. New Orleans. The machines under escort, and a day of one question.
  2. San Diego. Why California's governor's race was called so early: mail and early votes are counted first, so the first numbers aren't the whole picture. The only English-language explainer of the count we found that night.
  3. Philadelphia, in Spanish. When the polls close, how the count begins, when results might come, and a question to viewers about the Latino vote. Telemundo 62 carried both.
  4. New Jersey's 7th. Two reporters, two campaigns, one race, each pointing viewers to the other's feed. A newsroom using the feed as a feed.
  5. Reno. Which vote centers are open and how to vote, the morning of, then a check-in with viewers the morning after.
  6. Wilmington, N.C. A school board race told through a board member who wasn't on the ballot but ran ads and a website against a colleague. Accountability on the smallest race on the list.

The station is already carrying them

New Orleans was the exception. In 8 of the 9 markets where we checked the station's own accounts, the station shared, featured or echoed its young reporter's election posts. In New Jersey it split: one reporter's reels ran on the station's account, the other's didn't.

So the station and its young reporters are mostly working one feed already, and on most nights the station is glad to carry the reporter's post. What still sets a reporter's own account apart is voice: the direct address, the scene, the follow-up the next morning. That's what a station gets when it lets its newest people lead.

Where this fits

Nodes and Networks, the field study I wrote for ASU's Knight Center for the Future of News, found that governance shapes how big a journalism organization gets, and that creator brands rarely grow large on their own: 3 of the 26 in the study top $10 million. In election week we saw the creator form growing inside an old institution instead, borrowing a station's reach. In October's Watershed we found local information moving on vertical video and the open web in counties that had lost their papers. The same layer runs through these 12 markets, which aren't news deserts. Here, the station's own newest reporters run it.

The quiet

The best work was about process because that's what a reporter on the street adds on a night when the network already has the numbers. The quiet feeds say something too. About half the young reporters' feeds we opened had nothing on the election from Nov. 2 to 5, three of the biggest accounts we track among them. For most of these reporters a personal account isn't an election channel. It's a highlight reel, and the election didn't make the cut.

What nobody has written down

That split is the case for writing the deal. The ground-level work, how the machines move, how the count runs, what voters say on the sidewalk, is work stations get from their young reporters' own feeds and mostly don't publish on their websites. If stations want more of it, the people making it should know what they're building, and for whom. Two of seven big owner groups publish any rule on who owns a reporter's account, and no station job ad we read sets a follower count.

How we did this

We looked at on-air reporters at U.S. local stations who started in 2019 or later, and read their public Instagram and TikTok posts for Nov. 2 to 5 without following, liking or commenting. We shortlisted from what the posts explained, showed or did for viewers, then watched the best. For each reporter we read the station's own account and coded whether it carried, echoed or ignored the reporter's post. Station stories came from each station's politics or elections page. Independent pieces came from Pulse, the Atlas's weekly read of the creators it tracks. Every item was sorted into the same categories, written down before we started.

Method notes

Window and rules. Nov. 2 to 5, 2026, Eastern time unless noted. Public posts and pages only. Nobody was followed, liked or messaged. This preview carries counts and station headlines only: no reporter names, handles, post links or screenshots.

The six categories

Process
How voting or counting works: logistics, where and how to vote.
Place
On-scene local detail: voters, events, reaction, someone physically there.
Results
Who won, margins, calls.
National read
What it means beyond the market: Congress, parties, strategy.
Opinion
Prediction, take, argument, advocacy.
Other
Promotion, livestream notices, housekeeping.

One primary code per item. The codebook was written and time-stamped before any item was coded.

The four feeds

  • Young reporters (22 posts, 13 reporters, 12 markets). Shortlisted from more than 30 feeds, coded from the post descriptions. A best-case set picked for doing something well, not a sample.
  • Station websites (104 stories). Each of the 12 stations' politics or elections page, newest ten stories dated Nov. 2 to 5. Most coded from headlines; 18 unclear ones were re-read and re-coded; two false hits removed. Counts are a minimum and tilt toward Nov. 4 and 5.
  • Independents in the same states (60 pieces). Pulse pieces published Nov. 2 to 5 with election keywords in the title, by creators whose geography is one of the 12 election states, checked by hand. Matched by state, so several are national-style outlets. Mostly coded from titles.
  • National independents (300 pieces). The same filter for every other creator; a title-only read.

Agreement

A second coder, working blind, re-coded a random 20% of the three coded feeds and agreed 81% of the time. The process and place calls agreed on every item in the sample. Opinion and national read blur (69% agreement), so read them as one family.

Pulse share

Election-keyword headlines were 4.4% to 14.8% of Pulse pieces over four weeks (W42 64 of 1,441; W43 119 of 1,788; W44 205 of 2,311; W45 511 of 3,442), title keywords only. The last week is election week itself.

What this does not show

  • Volume. Station counts are capped, the reporters' set is chosen, and the independents are sorted by state.
  • That reporters are more local than stations in general. Station pages were read as listings.
  • What any station's social accounts did beyond the Instagram grids read for the station-pair check.
  • Silence as proof. A missing story, or an empty "no Pulse creator here" lane, is a gap in what we could see.
