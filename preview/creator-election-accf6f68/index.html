The Collab Button: Local TV's NIL Moment Showed Up on Election Night

On election week, local TV's newest reporters explained how the vote worked, on their own feeds. Their stations didn't remake the work. They signed on as co-authors.

Editor's note · Team

About this draft. Working draft 3, rebuilt around the Collab button; the earlier draft stays as it was. The lede rests on the HOLD below. Other headlines to try: "On Election Night, Local TV Hit Collab" · "The Station Signed Her Post" · "Local TV's NIL Moment Has a Button."

The day before Louisiana chose a U.S. senator, a young reporter at FOX 8 in New Orleans followed the voting machines out the door under police escort and told viewers what they needed to know: how the machines are secured, what's on the ballot, what the race is about. On Election Day she spent the day putting one question to voters, gave the people who politely passed the same care as the ones who answered, and set what she heard against a national survey.

She posted both on her own Instagram. Look at the names at the top of either post, though, and there's a second one: FOX 8. Instagram's Collab feature lets two accounts publish a single post, with the views, likes and comments pooled between them. The station didn't make its own version of the machines story or the one-question day. Its elections page that week ran 21 stories, a solid run of races and results, and neither of these was among them. It co-signed hers instead.

Editor's note · Justin

The paragraph above stands on the HOLD. Two cautions on it: the elections page lists only its latest 20 stories and starts the evening of Nov. 2, so "neither of these was among them" is a statement about that listing, not the whole window; and "pooled between them" is Instagram's own description of Collab (source: Instagram's help page, not yet opened).

That small move is happening all over local TV, and it's worth naming. College sports spent years treating athletes' followings as a threat, then flipped. Once athletes could earn from their name, image and likeness, the smart programs started helping them build their brands, because that's what recruits look for. Local TV is at the start of the same turn. Of 88 early-career on-air reporters the Atlas coded this fall, 76 post from personal accounts that name their station. On election week, in every market where we checked, the station carried at least some of that work: co-authored it, reshared it, or ran the same story the same way. Nobody announced a policy. The Collab button did it.

So we spent election week on their feeds. Here's how it looked, from the machines rolling out to the morning after.

What the week showed

The best work wasn't about who won. It was about how the vote happened: the machines, the vote centers, the count, one question on a sidewalk. That's what a reporter on the street adds on a night when the network already has the numbers. The stations' own websites ran mostly on races and results, about two of every three election stories. The independent creators the Atlas follows spent the week on what it all meant nationally.

The quiet was striking too. About half the young reporters' feeds we opened had nothing on the election at all, including three of the breakouts we track. Those are some of the biggest personal audiences in local TV, and through the biggest local story of the year they stayed on lifestyle posts, court stories and station promos. We don't know why yet. It's the question we most want to ask them.

Back to the button

The Collab button is a small thing. It's also the first draft of a deal. When FOX 8 co-signs a post, the station gets its young reporter's voice: the direct address, the scene, the follow-up the next morning, work it didn't make for its own website. The reporter gets the station's reach and its name on the work. On election week that trade happened across the markets we checked, without a meeting or a memo.

This is what local TV's NIL moment looks like at the start: not a policy, a habit. The college programs that leaned into their athletes' brands ended up recruiting on them. Stations can do the same, and without saying so, some already are. Every hire now arrives with an audience, though not one of the 47 station job ads we read asks for one. What a fair version of that deal looks like is a bigger question, and one we'll take up soon.

How we did this

We read the public Instagram and TikTok posts of on-air reporters at U.S. local stations who started in 2019 or later, for Nov. 2 to 5, without following, liking or commenting. We picked the best work by what it explained or showed viewers, watched it, and checked each station's own accounts to see whether it carried the post. Station stories came from each station's elections page, and the independents' work from Pulse, the Atlas's weekly read of the creators it tracks. The posts here are the best we found, not a sample.

Where the posts come from. The reporter posts are the shortlist, each embedded straight from Instagram. Nothing loads from Instagram until you click Load, and every placeholder has a plain link. How each station carried a post is proposed from the author line Instagram shows on the post and from our reads of station accounts, and each badge says pending until we rule. One post, a Chicago reporter's, has since been removed from Instagram, so it appears as a placeholder marked gone. The six independents in See the numbers are newsletters and sites, so they appear as links.

Method notes

Window and rules. Nov. 2 to 5, 2026, Eastern time. Public posts and pages only. Nobody was followed, liked or messaged, and no commenter or follower is named. Post dates and times are read from each post's own timestamp.

The six categories

Process
How voting or counting works: logistics, where and how to vote.
Place
On-scene local detail: voters, events, reaction, someone physically there.
Results
Who won, margins, calls.
National read
What it means beyond the market: Congress, parties, strategy.
Opinion
Prediction, take, argument, advocacy.
Other
Promotion, livestream notices, housekeeping.

One primary code per item. The codebook was written and time-stamped before any item was coded.

How a station carried a post

Co-authored
The station is listed in the post's author line on Instagram. Any post where the station is listed as co-author counts.
Reshared
The station's own account shows the reporter's post, but the author line doesn't list the station.
Same story
The station ran its own version of the same story or video.
Not carried
We checked, and the station's account doesn't show it.
Not checked
We didn't check the station's account.

Each badge is a proposal until we rule on it.

The feeds

  • Young reporters (22 posts, 13 reporters, 12 markets). Shortlisted from more than 30 feeds, coded from the post descriptions. A best-case set picked for doing something well, not a sample.
  • Station websites (104 stories). Each of the 12 stations' politics or elections page, newest ten stories dated Nov. 2 to 5. Most coded from headlines; 18 unclear ones were re-read and re-coded; two false hits removed. Counts are a minimum and tilt toward Nov. 4 and 5.
  • Independents in the same states (60 pieces). Pulse pieces published Nov. 2 to 5 with election keywords in the title, by creators whose geography is one of the 12 election states, checked by hand. Matched by state, so several are national-style outlets. Mostly coded from titles.
  • National independents (300 pieces). The same filter for every other creator; a title-only read.

What this does not show

  • Volume. Station counts are capped, the reporters' set is chosen, and the independents are sorted by state.
  • That reporters are more local than stations in general. Station pages were read as listings.
  • What any station's social accounts did beyond the Instagram reads behind the badges.
  • Silence as proof. A missing story, or an empty "no Pulse creator here" lane, is a gap in what we could see.
