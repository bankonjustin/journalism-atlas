Biggest by whose measure?

National outlets broke the biggest stories of the 2026 campaign. Of course they did. Below the top of the ballot, we traced 20 stories that changed a race back to the first report. Not one came from a national outlet.

Justin Bank

This summer, reporters at Louisville Public Media checked school board filings against newly redrawn district maps. Two candidates were running in districts they didn't live in. One withdrew. A judge struck the other from the race.

No national follow-up turned up in the outlets we could search. It still decided who represents thousands of families in Kentucky's largest school district.

The test is rigged

Last week in Poynter, Paul Farhi argued that national outlets landed the biggest stories of the 2026 campaign, and that this is a problem for local news. On his examples, he's right. The Times, the Journal, Politico, ProPublica and Mother Jones landed the decisive stories in the Senate and House races he lists.

But "biggest" is a circular test. A story gets big when national outlets, cable and podcasts pick it up. A national scoop in a state race is national news by definition — and because nearly all political news now travels nationally, it becomes the conversation back home, too. Grade local journalism on national attention and it loses every time.

So we used a different test. Not attention — consequence.

What we counted

We collected 31 accountability stories from 2026 races in 18 states where something concrete happened. A candidate withdrew. A court disqualified someone. An official resigned. A grand jury indicted. An ethics complaint was filed. We capped federal and statewide races at 40% of the set so the rest of the ballot couldn't get crowded out. Then we traced every story back to its earliest public report, in any format, and only then looked at who published it.

It's a pilot, not a census. It's big enough to show the pattern that national attention hides.

The level of the race predicts who breaks the story

Below federal and statewide races, all 20 first reports came from inside the state. Half came from the same city or county. National outlets show up only at the top of the ballot — and even there, they made four of 11 first reports.

Farhi looked where national outlets are strongest

Put his examples next to ours and the selection problem is plain. In his nine national stories, a national outlet published the core allegation first eight times. In our federal and statewide events, four of 11. Below that: zero of 20.

Who broke them

The outlets doing this work aren't the metro dailies whose decline usually stands in for "local news." Two public radio stations. A county weekly. A nonprofit statehouse service. A small investigative nonprofit in Utah. A daily that still sends reporters after police reports. None of these stories was big in Farhi's sense. Every one had a consequence voters could see.

Allegations start everywhere now. Newsrooms still decide what's real

Newsrooms made 19 of the 31 first reports. The rest started somewhere else: a court filing, a county announcement, a PAC's opposition research, a caucus's X account, an opponent's videos, a homeowner's doorbell clip on Facebook. Only one came from a journalist-run blog.

That's the new front end. The back end is where local journalism earns its keep — check the claim, find the record, get the admission, decide what's real. In 10 of 31 events the trigger was an institution, and the local outlet's job was making it public. The Platte County Landmark did that for a county commissioner under state police investigation. The Utah Investigative Journalism Project did it for a sheriff a grand jury later indicted. Farhi's frame counts only exclusives that topple someone. Most local accountability is watching institutions and telling voters what they're doing.

It runs both ways

All news is national now, but traffic moves in both directions. The San Francisco Chronicle reported a former staffer's assault allegation against Eric Swalwell, and he left the California governor's race; CNN published an allegation from the same source the same day. The Milwaukee Journal Sentinel published concerns about Mandela Barnes's behavior toward young women on the day he left the Wisconsin governor's race. He says he left for political and financial reasons, and he has sued the paper.

And the other way: when ProPublica reported that FBI agents had been looking at Sen. Susan Collins, the Bangor Daily News ran ProPublica's follow-up on its own site. That isn't local journalism failing. It's a network — and the credit goes to whoever is biggest when the story arrives.

Where Farhi is right

We traced his examples too. On federal races, his case mostly holds. In four of his nine stories, a local or state outlet had already published the allegation or the facts underneath it before the outlet he credits. In only one, the Mills story, did a local paper publish the core allegation first. Correcting his attributions cuts both ways. The Journal beat the Times on the Platner texts. TMZ had the Max Miller allegations before Mother Jones. A Daytona Beach paper printed a rival's stolen-valor accusations against Rep. Cory Mills more than a year before Mother Jones did.

The sharpest finding cuts against local news. Sen. Roger Marshall's lawsuits against former patients sat in Kansas court files for years. In the archives we could search, no Kansas outlet reported them before the Times did. Defense-contractor donations to Collins began nearly a year before The Intercept connected them. The records were public. Nobody local read them.

What's actually missing

Local reporters haven't stopped finding things. Below the top of the ballot, every first report in our pilot came from inside the state, including all 11 that came from newsrooms. What's missing is narrower, and fixable.

Records. Tips flow to prestige outlets, and the Marshall story started with one. Records are open to anyone who reads them. Nobody reads them systematically, across states, for every name on the ballot. That's work you could do once and share across a network of newsrooms. CalMatters' Digital Democracy shows what it looks like for one statehouse.

Risk. Of our 31 events, 15 are disputed and six are in litigation. A national outlet can absorb a lawsuit. A county weekly may not survive one. Farhi is right that distance protects national reporters. So share the protection — legal defense, insurance, pre-publication review — across the outlets doing the local work.

The biggest stories of 2026 broke nationally, because that's what biggest means. The stories that changed who was on the ballot broke close to home.

How we did this

Two datasets, with rules written before we looked at results and a required section for evidence against our own view.

Farhi's examples. We traced his nine national stories, two regional counter-examples and the 2022 George Santos case back to the earliest public reporting, coding what had been published before the outlet he credits ran its story.

The consequence pilot. We collected 2026 withdrawals, disqualifications, resignations, indictments and formal complaints across 18 states and every level of office, and traced each to its earliest public item. Federal and statewide races are 11 of 31. Appointments and resignations from seats not on the ballot are included and flagged. A complaint filed by a partisan counts only if an agency acted on it; one event failed that test and was dropped.

Limits. Starting from consequences means we see only stories that changed something. Stories that ran and changed nothing are invisible, and so are races nobody covered. Search tools index small outlets, Facebook groups and newsletters poorly, which probably undercounts local and independent first reports. Some publishers block our tools, so for them we relied on syndicated copies and linking outlets; we never bypassed a paywall. Print archives were not searched, and the Marshall archive check is still open.

Disclosure. Justin Bank co-founded the Independent Journalism Atlas, which tracks independent and local news outlets.
