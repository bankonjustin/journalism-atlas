The 2026 election, below the federal level

Local newsrooms didn’t initially report most of the scandals. But they amplified them and saw them through.

A recent Poynter column was right that national outlets broke the big congressional stories. We traced 43 more, from governor down to school board, that ended in a withdrawal, a disqualification, a resignation or a charge. Fewer than two in five started in a newsroom. But four in five ended with a local or statewide newsroom carrying the story to its conclusion.

Justin Bank

In May, a Louisville homeowner posted doorbell footage to Facebook. It showed a man taking a campaign mailer for state Rep. Daniel Grossberg out of the mailbox and putting it in his pocket. The man was Max Morley, Grossberg’s challenger in the primary. Kentucky Public Radio and the Courier-Journal picked it up. Morley admitted it on May 12 and suspended his campaign the next day.

No newsroom started that story. Newsrooms in Kentucky put it in front of voters, and it was over within days, with almost no one outside the state paying attention. That is the shape of most of what we found below the federal level in 2026.

Races for Congress were different. In a Nov. 12 column for Poynter, Paul Farhi argued that national outlets broke the stories that decided 2026’s marquee Senate and House races, while local news was often “the dog that didn’t, and perhaps couldn’t, hunt.” For Congress, our numbers back him up. Six of the nine congressional stories we traced started outside the state, most of them at national or foreign outlets. Congress is where national newsrooms keep their reporters.

Below Congress, the national press mostly wasn’t there. Every story but one started inside the state, and that one began at a Providence TV station covering New Bedford, part of its own market across the Massachusetts line.

So instead of asking who was first, we asked two things about each of the 43 stories below Congress: where it started, and who was still reporting it when it ended. Only 16 started with a reporter. The rest began with a court filing, a sheriff’s statement, an opponent’s research or a candidate’s own video. But in four of every five, a local or statewide newsroom was on the story when the candidate withdrew, was disqualified, resigned or was charged.

If anything, those counts understate how much starts close to home. Search tools see national sites well and county weeklies, Facebook groups and creators’ feeds poorly, so those are the starts most likely to slip past a review like ours.

Where they started

Below Congress, allegations came from almost anywhere. In Palm Beach County, the first public word that the county clerk had been arrested came from Florida’s attorney general, in a post on X. In Knox County, Tennessee, the first word of the indictments in the sheriff’s primary came from the sitting sheriff, in a statement to local reporters a day before the grand jury’s charges were announced.

In South Carolina, a state representative’s primary opponent livestreamed the polling-place confrontation that led to his arrest. Six of the 43 began with an opponent, a party or a PAC. That is opposition research doing what campaigns pay it to do: the people most motivated to read a candidate’s records are the people running against them. In 7 more, several items went up the same day and we couldn’t tell which came first, as the table shows.

Three began with the candidates themselves, and we kept them on purpose. In Portland, a City Council candidate announced on Instagram that he was leaving the race, days after the city’s elections office told him he didn’t meet its residency requirements; Willamette Week reported it.

The one creator case in our set is Eric Swalwell’s. The San Francisco Chronicle and CNN published the first detailed allegations against him on April 10. Before that, the accusations had circulated on social media, including in a March 31 video by the creator Cheyenne Hunt. Swalwell ended his campaign for governor two days after the detailed allegations ran. He denies them.

Starting a story turned out to be the easy part; getting voters to weigh it is where newsrooms came in.

How they ended

Of the 27 stories that started outside a newsroom, a local or statewide newsroom was on 20 by the end.

In July, reporters at Louisville Public Media checked the addresses of Jefferson County school board candidates against newly redrawn district maps. Two of them didn’t live in the districts they were running to represent. By late September, a judge had struck one from the ballot and the other had withdrawn. No national outlet followed, but the reporting still decided who sits on the board of Kentucky’s largest school district.

In Wilmington, North Carolina, WHQR got a tip on primary night, checked it against the sex offender registry and federal court records, and reported that school board candidate Rick Southerland lived and worked with a registered sex offender. The county Democratic Party called on him to quit, and he dropped out the next day.

In Platte City, Missouri, the weekly Platte County Landmark reported that state police were investigating the county’s presiding commissioner, Scott Fricker. He withdrew from the race three weeks later. No charges have been reported as of Nov. 18, and Fricker and the special prosecutor are in a dispute, so we describe an investigation, not a finding.

In Utah, a small nonprofit, the Utah Investigative Journalism Project, reported in April that the Wasatch County sheriff, Jared Rigby, was under criminal investigation over an anonymous letter attacking his primary challenger. A grand jury indicted him in September. The case is pending.

Capitol News Illinois first reported on state Rep. Harry Benton in February; the state’s legislative inspector general followed, and Benton resigned in July. The Atlanta Journal-Constitution filed a public records request with Nevada and reported that a Georgia House nominee, David Dillie, may have voted in two states’ primaries; Georgia’s secretary of state moved to take him off the ballot. Dillie disputes it.

Look at who those newsrooms were. Of the 16 stories below Congress that a newsroom started, only four came from a metro daily. Three came from public radio. Two came from local TV. The rest came from the independent layer, from a county weekly in Missouri to a nonprofit in Utah and a blog in Oklahoma.

By the end, independent outlets were on 16 of the 34 stories that in-state newsrooms carried. Local TV started only two of these stories. By the end it was on 12. The people starting accountability stories below Congress mostly work outside the institutions that get credited for them. That is the part of the ecosystem the Independent Journalism Atlas was built to map.

None of that is a scoop in the sense Farhi means. Some of it began with a court filing or a police investigation. The newsroom’s job was to check it and put it in front of the people voting. Below Congress, that is most of what accountability looks like, and the work a count of scoops can’t see.

Up at the federal level, the chain often ran the other way, and the outlet at the end of it got the credit.

When a story went national

Politico had the first allegation of sexual assault against Graham Platner, which he denies. The Daily Mail had the Oklahoma candidate Jackson Lahmeyer’s texts before any Tulsa outlet.

His nine examples mostly hold up. In eight of the nine, a national outlet did publish the allegation first. In three, though, that outlet wasn’t the one he credits. The Wall Street Journal reported Platner’s texts hours before the Times. TMZ had the Max Miller allegations three months before Mother Jones. And the Daytona Beach News-Journal printed a rival’s stolen-valor accusations against Rep. Cory Mills in August 2024. Mother Jones got to them a year and a half later.

In four of the nine, local or in-state outlets had already published the allegation or the records underneath it. The Portland Press Herald and Honolulu Civil Beat reported the donations behind the Collins and Navatek story in February 2020. The Bangor Daily News item Farhi credits came two years later.

The finding that should sting local news runs the other way. Sen. Roger Marshall’s lawsuits against more than 700 former patients, by the Times’s count, sat in Kansas court files until Sarah Kliff of the Times, acting on a tip, pulled them. We found no earlier coverage online, though we couldn’t search the print archives of small Kansas papers.

Defense-contractor donations to Susan Collins sat in federal election filings for nearly a year before The Intercept tied them to an Army truck appropriation. The records were public, and we found no local outlet that had read them.

Credit goes to whoever is biggest when the story arrives

When national outlets followed local reporting, they rarely said so. Mother Jones’s February investigation of Rep. Cory Mills drew on the Daytona Beach News-Journal’s 2024 reporting on his military record, and cited it as “a 2024 Florida newspaper article.” ABC’s coverage of Tony Gonzales didn’t name the San Antonio Express-News. Fox News did, and linked to it.

We checked 9 stories where a national outlet followed earlier local or in-state reporting. We could read the national pieces in 7. In 3 of those, at least one linked back. That is too few stories to make much of the exact number. It is enough to see the habit Farhi’s column reproduces: the outlet at the end of the chain gets remembered as the start of it.

What would help: records and legal cover

Local reporters haven’t stopped finding things. That’s not where the gap is. What’s thin is narrower and fixable: the hours to read records before an opponent does, and the protection to publish what turns up.

Records. Campaigns pay opposition researchers to read court dockets, campaign filings and property records for every opponent, and six of our stories below Congress started that way. Newsrooms rarely have those hours, and almost never across state lines. The Journal-Constitution’s Nevada records request is what it looks like when one does. Marshall’s court files and Collins’s donations are what gets missed when no one does.

This is work that could be done once and shared: a records scan of every name on a state’s ballot, run for a network of newsrooms, every cycle. CalMatters’ Digital Democracy already does a version of it for one statehouse.

Staffing is the other limit. Alex Seitz-Wald, deputy editor of the Midcoast Villager, told CJR, “I have four full-time reporters, total.” Kate Cough, editor of the Maine Monitor, pushed back in the same piece. She pointed to her paper’s own Platner profile and other Maine outlets’ coverage, and called it less “media blindness” than “different audiences, framing, and differing reporting priorities” (CJR, “Out of State”). The scale of the gap is in the Medill State of Local News and in our essay on news deserts.

Legal cover. Of our 52 stories, 20 are disputed and 19 involve pending court cases, most of them criminal. Mandela Barnes has sued the Milwaukee Journal Sentinel, which published concerns about his behavior toward young women on the day he left Wisconsin’s governor’s race; he says he left for political and financial reasons.

A national outlet can absorb a suit like that. A county weekly might not survive one. Pool the protection: legal defense, libel insurance and pre-publication review, shared across the outlets doing the local work. The Reporters Committee’s Local Legal Initiative offers free legal help to local journalists, and it is a model worth scaling.

Nobody on a national desk was going to notice that two Louisville school board candidates lived on the wrong side of a new district line. Someone at Louisville Public Media did, and checked.

How we did this

Two sets of stories, built with the same rules: Farhi’s examples and the 52 2026 races that ended in a concrete consequence.

Farhi’s examples. We traced his nine national stories, two regional counter-examples and the 2022 George Santos case back to the earliest public reporting, coding what had been published before the outlet he credits ran its story.

Our review of 52 races. We collected 2026 withdrawals, disqualifications, resignations, indictments, charges and formal complaints in 33 states and at every level of office. We started with a pilot of 31, then widened the search for local TV stories, state election records, statehouse news services and ethics committees. A person had to be in a 2026 race (appointments are left out) and face a concrete result. A complaint from a partisan counts only if an agency acted on it. We left out four: a sheriff whose seat was not on the 2026 ballot, a congressional candidate whose complaint drew no agency action, and two edge cases we couldn’t cleanly place in a 2026 race, a judicial appointment and a sheriff’s resignation. All four remain in the table, flagged. We capped the top of the ballot at 40 percent so national races wouldn’t dominate the sample; the pattern below Congress doesn’t depend on that cap. We traced each story to the earliest public item in any format and only then looked at who published it. In 3 of the 43 stories below Congress, that item was the candidate’s own disclosure. We coded "who made it stick" for all 43: whether an in-state news outlet was carrying the story when the consequence came. We count any news outlet as a newsroom, whatever its lean. One first item, Iowa Starting Line, comes from a Democratic-leaning site owned by Courier Newsroom; counting it as a party source instead would move one row and change no geography. As a sensitivity check, 10 of the 43 are weaker cases, because someone was charged but stayed in the race. Without them 33 remain, one started out of state and 15 started in the same city or county.

Limits. Starting from results means we see only stories that brought a consequence. Stories that ran and changed nothing are invisible, and so are races nobody covered. Search tools index small outlets, Facebook groups and newsletters poorly, which probably undercounts local and independent first items. Some publishers (AP, the Times, the Journal, Politico, several local papers) block our tools, so for them we relied on syndicated copies and linking outlets. We never bypassed a paywall, and some links in the table could not be checked automatically. In 7 stories we could not tell which of several same-day items came first. Several charged-but-still-running cases brought a smaller consequence than a withdrawal does; we kept them under our rule, and the pattern holds without them. Local TV had the first item in three of our 52 stories. We could not search print archives, so the Marshall check covers only what is online.
