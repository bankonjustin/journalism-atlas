Outside Congress, every accountability story we traced started in-state.

National outlets won the Congress scoops: out-of-state sources had it first in 7 of 10, five of them national or foreign outlets. Across 46 races from governor to school board, none started out of state.

Justin Bank

Last week in Poynter, Paul Farhi made a case that's hard to argue with on its own terms. National outlets broke the stories that decided 2026's marquee Senate and House races, he wrote, and the local news media was often "the dog that didn't, and perhaps couldn't, hunt."

A national scoop in a state race is national news by definition, so grading local news by what goes national means it always loses. Grade it by what came of it, and every story outside Congress had a result voters could see.

We wanted to know whether Farhi's pattern holds beyond Congress. We gathered 56 stories from 33 states that brought a concrete consequence, such as a candidate quitting, a judge striking someone from the ballot or a sheriff being indicted. Then we traced each one to the first time it appeared in public.

It holds for Congress. Outside it, none of the 46 started out of state, and an in-state news outlet was carrying the story when the consequence came in 37 of them.

Start in Louisville. This summer, reporters at Louisville Public Media checked school board filings against newly redrawn district maps. Two candidates were running in districts they didn't live in. One withdrew. A judge struck the other from the race. We found no national follow-up. It still decided who represents thousands of families in Kentucky's largest school district.

In Wilmington, North Carolina, WHQR got a tip on primary night. Reporters checked it against the sex offender registry and federal court records. They reported that school board candidate Rick Southerland lived and worked with a registered sex offender. The county Democratic party called on him to quit, and he dropped out the next day.

In Platte City, Missouri, the weekly Platte County Landmark reported that state police were investigating the county's presiding commissioner, Scott Fricker. He later withdrew from the race. No charges have been reported as of Nov. 17, and Fricker and the prosecutor are in a dispute, so we treat the investigation as an allegation.

In Utah, a small nonprofit, the Utah Investigative Journalism Project, reported that the Wasatch County sheriff, Jared Rigby, was under criminal investigation over an anonymous letter attacking his primary challenger. A grand jury later indicted him. The case is pending.

Local TV is here too. In Georgia, Atlanta News First reported on the Towns County sheriff and a call for a special prosecutor, and Sheriff Kenneth Henderson was later indicted.

Where Farhi is right

National outlets do own one corner of the map: Congress. Seven of our 10 Congress stories started out of state, and nothing outside Congress comes close. Politico published the allegation of sexual assault against Graham Platner first, which he denies. The Daily Mail had the Oklahoma congressional candidate Jackson Lahmeyer's texts before any Tulsa outlet. In Farhi's own nine stories, a national outlet had it first 8 times.

Farhi looked where national outlets are strongest. That's a fair place to look. It just isn't the whole ballot. And one of his own examples runs our way: he credits the San Antonio Express-News and the Texas Tribune with breaking the Tony Gonzales story. Gonzales left his runoff in March and resigned in April. A San Antonio outlet, Current Revolt, ran the first unsourced rumor in September 2025, and the Express-News published the first text message in February.

We traced his nine stories too, and found a few he missed. In four of them, a local or in-state outlet had already published the allegation or the facts underneath it. The Portland Press Herald and Honolulu Civil Beat reported the donations behind the Collins and Navatek story in February 2020, two years before the Bangor Daily News item Farhi credits with the first whiff. A Daytona Beach paper printed a rival's stolen-valor accusations against Rep. Cory Mills more than a year before Mother Jones did. Correcting his credits cuts both ways: the Journal published the Platner texts before the Times, and TMZ had the Max Miller allegations before Mother Jones.

The sharpest finding cuts against local news. Sen. Roger Marshall's lawsuits against former patients sat in Kansas court files for years. We found no earlier coverage online, though we could not search the print archives of local papers, and we could not verify when the filings began, so Marshall is not on the chart. Kansas outlets did original work once the Times set the agenda, including Kansas Reflector's reporting on the class action. Defense-contractor donations to Collins began nearly a year before The Intercept connected them. The records were public. We found no local outlet that connected them.

Allegations start anywhere now. Newsrooms still decide what's real

A newsroom had it first in 18 of the 46 stories outside Congress. The rest started somewhere else: a court filing, a county announcement, a PAC's opposition research, a caucus's X account, an opponent's livestream, a homeowner's doorbell clip on Facebook. In 7 of our 56 stories we couldn't pin down which came first, and we say so in the table.

The back end is where local journalism earns its keep: check the claim, find the record, get the admission, decide what's real. When a court, prosecutor or agency moved first, the local outlet's job was often making it public. The Platte County Landmark did that for a county commissioner under investigation. The Utah Investigative Journalism Project did it for a sheriff a grand jury later indicted. Farhi's frame counts only exclusives that topple someone. Most local accountability is watching institutions and telling voters what they're doing.

Credit: who gets named when a story travels

All news is national now, and credit tends to go to whoever is biggest when a story arrives. So when a national outlet follows local reporting, does it say so? We checked the national follow-ups to stories that local or in-state outlets had touched first.

Mother Jones used a Daytona Beach newspaper's 2024 reporting on Rep. Cory Mills's stolen-valor claims, but called it only "a 2024 Florida newspaper article," with no name and no link. Its Max Miller story named no Ohio outlet. On the Tony Gonzales story, ABC did not name the San Antonio Express-News. Fox did, and linked it.

On Eric Swalwell, NBC, AP, the Washington Free Beacon and Mediaite all linked the San Francisco Chronicle. The Chronicle and CNN published the first detailed allegations on April 10. Before that, the accusations had spread on social media, including a March 31 video by the creator Cheyenne Hunt. Swalwell ended his campaign two days after the detailed allegations ran, and he denies them. The Milwaukee Journal Sentinel published concerns about Mandela Barnes's behavior toward young women on the day he left the Wisconsin governor's race. He says he left for political and financial reasons, and he has sued the paper.

Counting by story, not by follow-up: of the 7 stories where we could read the national pieces, 3 linked back to the earlier reporting. Those were Collins, Swalwell and Gonzales. No link came on Mills, Miller, Cherfilus-McCormick or Stewart, though the Cherfilus-McCormick pieces were resignation stories and a weak test. Credit goes toward the biggest name.

What's missing: records and legal risk

Local reporters haven't stopped finding things. Outside Congress, every first item in our review came from inside the state, including all 18 that came from newsrooms. What's missing is narrower, and fixable.

Records. Tips flow to prestige outlets, and the Marshall story started with one. Records are open to anyone who reads them. Nobody reads them systematically, across states, for every name on the ballot. That's work you could do once and share across a network of newsrooms. CalMatters' Digital Democracy shows what it looks like for one statehouse. Staffing is the other limit. Alex Seitz-Wald, deputy editor of the Midcoast Villager, told CJR, "I have four full-time reporters, total." Kate Cough, editor of the Maine Monitor, pushed back in the same piece. She pointed to her paper's own Platner profile and to heavy coverage from other Maine outlets, and said it was less "media blindness" than "different audiences, framing, and differing reporting priorities" (CJR, "Out of State"). The scale of the gap is in the Medill State of Local News and in our own essay on news deserts.

Risk. Of our 56 stories, 23 are disputed and 19 are in litigation. A national outlet can absorb a lawsuit. A county weekly may not survive one. Farhi is right that distance protects national reporters. So share the protection: legal defense, insurance and pre-publication review across the outlets doing the local work. The Reporters Committee's Local Legal Initiative offers free legal help to local journalists.

How we did this

Two sets of stories, built with the same rules: Farhi's examples and the 2026 races that changed.

Farhi's examples. We traced his nine national stories, two regional counter-examples and the 2022 George Santos case back to the earliest public reporting, coding what had been published before the outlet he credits ran its story.

Our review of 56 races. We collected 2026 withdrawals, disqualifications, resignations, indictments, charges and formal complaints in 33 states and at every level of office. We started with a pilot of 31, then widened the search for local TV stories, state election records, statehouse news services and ethics committees. A person had to be in a 2026 race (appointments and seats off the ballot are flagged) and face a concrete result. A complaint from a partisan counts only if an agency acted on it; one event failed that test and was dropped. We kept the top of the ballot (Congress, governor and the other statewide offices) under 40 percent of the set. We traced each story to the earliest public item in any format and only then looked at who published it. In 3 of the 46 stories outside Congress, that item was the candidate's own disclosure. We coded "who made it stick" for all 46: whether an in-state news outlet was carrying the story when the consequence came. We count any news outlet as a newsroom, whatever its lean. One first item, Iowa Starting Line, comes from a Democratic-leaning site owned by Courier Newsroom; counting it as a party source instead would move one row and change no geography. As a sensitivity check, 10 of the 46 are weaker cases, because someone was charged but stayed in the race. Without them 36 remain, none started out of state and 17 started in the same city or county.

Limits. Starting from results means we see only stories that brought a consequence. Stories that ran and changed nothing are invisible, and so are races nobody covered. Search tools index small outlets, Facebook groups and newsletters poorly, which probably undercounts local and independent first items. Some publishers (AP, the Times, the Journal, Politico, several local papers) block our tools, so for them we relied on syndicated copies and linking outlets. We never bypassed a paywall, and some links in the table could not be checked automatically. In 7 stories we could not tell which of several same-day items came first. Several charged-but-still-running cases brought a smaller consequence than a withdrawal does; we kept them under our rule, and the pattern holds without them. Local TV had the first item in four of our 56 stories, three of them from an arrest or an indictment, and the Georgia sheriff's seat wasn't on this year's ballot, one of the looser fits in our set. We could not search print archives, so the Marshall check covers only what is online.
