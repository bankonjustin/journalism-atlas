Review copy. Not for circulation. November 18, 2026.

Below the federal level, 2026’s scandals didn’t need a scoop. They needed a local reporter.

We traced where scandals started, and who carried them to a consequence.

Paul Farhi’s Poynter piece is right that national outlets broke the big congressional stories. We traced 43 more, from governor down to school board, that ended in a withdrawal, a disqualification, a resignation or a charge. Fewer than half started in a newsroom. But four in five ended with an in-state newsroom on the story.

Justin Bank

In May, a Louisville homeowner posted doorbell footage to Facebook. It showed a man lifting a campaign mailer for state Rep. Daniel Grossberg out of the mailbox and putting it in his pocket. The man was Max Morley, who was challenging Grossberg in the primary. Kentucky Public Radio and the Courier-Journal picked it up. Morley admitted it on May 12 and suspended his campaign the next day.

It’s a small story, and a useful one, because it shows how most of 2026’s campaign scandals actually played out below Congress. A private citizen surfaced it. Local reporters confirmed it and put it in front of voters. It ended his campaign within days.

That isn’t the story Paul Farhi told in his Nov. 12 column for Poynter. National outlets, he wrote, broke the stories that decided 2026’s marquee Senate and House races, while local news was often “the dog that didn’t, and perhaps couldn’t, hunt.” For Congress, our numbers mostly back him up.

But the scoop is the last step in a longer process, and the one most likely to come with a byline people remember. Before it, someone surfaces an allegation: a reporter, a court clerk, an opponent, a neighbor with a doorbell camera. Someone checks it. Then something happens. Farhi asked who was first to the biggest stories. We wanted to know who did the rest of the work.

So we went looking. We collected 52 2026 races in 33 states where a candidate or officeholder withdrew, was disqualified, resigned or was charged, and traced each one back to the first place the allegation appeared in public. Below Congress, only 16 of 43 started in a newsroom. The rest began with a court filing, a sheriff’s press release, a PAC’s opposition research, a candidate’s own video. But in 34 of the 43, a newsroom in the same state was on the story by the time it ended a campaign or brought a charge.

If anything, those counts understate how much starts close to home. Search tools index national sites well and small outlets, Facebook groups and newsletters poorly. The stories most likely to slip past a review like ours are the ones that began in a county weekly, a local Facebook group or a creator’s feed.

Allegations start almost anywhere now. Confirming them is still local work, and it’s the work a count of scoops leaves out.

Congress is the exception

Farhi is right about Congress. Six of the nine congressional stories in our set started outside the state, five of them at national or foreign outlets. Politico had the first allegation of sexual assault against Graham Platner, which he denies. The Daily Mail had the Oklahoma candidate Jackson Lahmeyer’s texts before any Tulsa outlet. Congress is where national newsrooms keep their reporters, and it showed.

His nine examples mostly hold up, too. In eight of the nine, a national outlet did publish the allegation first. In three, though, that outlet wasn’t the one he credits. The Wall Street Journal reported Platner’s texts hours before the Times. TMZ reported the custody-fight allegations against Rep. Max Miller three months before Mother Jones. And the Daytona Beach News-Journal printed a rival’s stolen-valor accusations against Rep. Cory Mills in August 2024, a year and a half before Mother Jones got to them.

In four of the nine, local or in-state outlets had already published the allegation or the records underneath it. The Portland Press Herald and Honolulu Civil Beat reported the donations behind the Collins and Navatek story in February 2020, two years before the Bangor Daily News item Farhi credits.

The finding that should sting local news runs the other way. Sen. Roger Marshall’s lawsuits against more than 700 former patients, by the Times’s count, sat in Kansas court files until Sarah Kliff of the Times pulled them. We found no earlier coverage online, though we couldn’t search the print archives of small Kansas papers. Defense-contractor donations to Susan Collins sat in federal election filings for nearly a year before The Intercept tied them to an Army truck appropriation. The records were public in both cases. We found no local outlet that had read them.

Allegations start anywhere now

Only 16 of the 43 stories below Congress started with a reporter. The rest came from everywhere else. In Palm Beach County, the first public word that the clerk of court had been arrested came from Florida’s attorney general, in a post on X. In Knox County, Tennessee, the first word that two candidates who had lost the sheriff’s primary were under indictment came from the sitting sheriff, in a statement to local reporters the night before state investigators announced the charges. In South Carolina, a state representative’s primary opponent livestreamed the polling-place confrontation that led to his arrest. In North Carolina, the first word came from the state House Republican caucus’s X account. In Texas, it was a PAC.

Six of the 43 began with an opponent, a party or a PAC. That is opposition research doing what campaigns pay it to do, and it is worth sitting with: the people most motivated to read a candidate’s records are the people running against them. In 7 more, several items went up the same day and we couldn’t tell which came first. The table says so.

Three began with the candidates themselves, and we kept them on purpose. In Portland, a City Council candidate announced on Instagram that he was leaving the race, days after the city’s elections office told him he didn’t meet its residency and voter-registration requirements. He disputes part of that finding; Willamette Week reported it. In New Hampshire, a state Senate nominee said in a video that he had realized he didn’t meet the state’s seven-year residency requirement. In Wisconsin, Lt. Gov. Sara Rodriguez disclosed that she had fired her campaign manager over double-counted donations, then ended her run for governor five days later. In each case the candidate’s announcement was the first public word, and it came because something on paper had stopped adding up.

The one creator case in our set is Eric Swalwell’s. Before the San Francisco Chronicle and CNN published the first detailed allegations against him on April 10, the accusations had been circulating on social media, including in a March 31 video by the creator Cheyenne Hunt. Swalwell ended his campaign for governor two days after the detailed allegations ran. He denies them.

A homeowner in Louisville and a creator in California could put an allegation into the world. In both places, it took a newsroom to turn it into something voters could weigh.

Newsrooms still decide what’s real

In July, reporters at Louisville Public Media checked the addresses of Jefferson County school board candidates against newly redrawn district maps. Two of them didn’t live in the districts they were running to represent. By late September, a judge had struck one from the ballot and the other had withdrawn. No national outlet followed. It still decided who sits on the board of Kentucky’s largest school district.

In Wilmington, North Carolina, WHQR got a tip on primary night. Reporters checked it against the sex offender registry and federal court records, then reported that school board candidate Rick Southerland lived and worked with a registered sex offender. The county Democratic Party called on him to quit. He dropped out the next day.

In Platte City, Missouri, the weekly Platte County Landmark reported that state police were investigating the county’s presiding commissioner, Scott Fricker. He withdrew from the race three weeks later. No charges had been reported as of June, when Fricker said the investigation was over and the special prosecutor said it remained open. We describe an investigation, not a finding.

In Utah, a small nonprofit, the Utah Investigative Journalism Project, reported in April that the Wasatch County sheriff, Jared Rigby, was under criminal investigation over an anonymous letter attacking his primary challenger. A grand jury indicted him in September. He has pleaded not guilty, and the case is pending.

The pattern runs down the table. The Albuquerque Journal’s reporting on state Rep. Joseph Hernandez came three weeks before he withdrew. The Atlanta Journal-Constitution filed a public records request with Nevada and reported that records there showed a Georgia House nominee, David Dillie, voting in Nevada’s June primary. He denies it. Georgia’s secretary of state moved to take him off the ballot; a judge kept him on, and he won in November.

Look at who those newsrooms were. Of the 16 stories below Congress that a newsroom started, only four came from a metro daily. Three came from public radio. Two came from local TV. The rest came from the independent layer: a county weekly in Missouri, a small investigative nonprofit in Utah, two nonprofit statehouse services, a digital newsroom in San Francisco, an Oklahoma blog and a partisan-leaning Iowa site. Add the creator and the homeowner who first surfaced the Swalwell and Morley stories. By the end, independent outlets were on 17 of the 34 stories that in-state newsrooms carried. Local TV started only two of these stories but was on 12 by the end. The people starting accountability stories below Congress mostly work outside the institutions that get credited for them. That is the part of the ecosystem the Independent Journalism Atlas was built to map.

None of that is a scoop in the sense Farhi means. Some of it began with a court filing or a police investigation that already existed. The newsroom’s job was to check it, find the record and put it in front of the people voting. Below Congress, that is most of what accountability looks like, and it is the work a count of scoops can’t see.

Credit goes to whoever is biggest when the story arrives

When national outlets followed local reporting, they rarely said so. Mother Jones’s February investigation of Rep. Cory Mills drew on the Daytona Beach News-Journal’s 2024 reporting on his military record, and cited it as “a 2024 Florida newspaper article.” No name, no link. Its Max Miller story named no Ohio outlet. ABC’s coverage of Tony Gonzales didn’t name the San Antonio Express-News. Fox News did, and linked to it.

We checked 9 stories where a national outlet followed earlier local or in-state reporting. We could read the national pieces in 7, and in 3 at least one of them linked back. That is too few stories to make much of the exact number. It is enough to see the habit Farhi’s column reproduces: the outlet at the end of the chain gets remembered as the start of it.

What would help: records and legal cover

Local reporters haven’t stopped finding things. Below Congress, every story but one started inside the state, and that one began at a Providence TV station covering New Bedford, its own market across the Massachusetts line. What’s thin is narrower and fixable: the hours to read records before an opponent does, and the protection to publish what turns up.

Records. Campaigns pay opposition researchers to read court dockets, campaign filings and property records for every opponent, and six of our stories below Congress started that way. Newsrooms rarely have those hours, and almost never across state lines. The Journal-Constitution’s Nevada records request is what it looks like when one does. Marshall’s court files and Collins’s donations are what gets missed when no one does. This is work that could be done once and shared: a records scan of every name on a state’s ballot, run for a network of newsrooms, every cycle. CalMatters’ Digital Democracy already does a version of it for one statehouse.

Staffing is the other limit. Alex Seitz-Wald, deputy editor of the Midcoast Villager, told CJR, “I have four full-time reporters, total.” Kate Cough, editor of the Maine Monitor, pushed back in the same piece. She pointed to her paper’s own Platner profile and to heavy coverage from other Maine outlets, and called it less “media blindness” than “different audiences, framing, and differing reporting priorities” (CJR, “Out of State”). The scale of the gap is in the Medill State of Local News and in our essay on news deserts.

Legal cover. Of our 52 stories, 20 are disputed and 19 involve pending court cases, most of them criminal. Mandela Barnes has sued the Milwaukee Journal Sentinel, which published concerns about his behavior toward young women on the day he left Wisconsin’s governor’s race; he says he left for political and financial reasons. A national outlet can absorb a suit like that. A county weekly might not survive one. Pool the protection: legal defense, libel insurance and pre-publication review, shared across the outlets doing the local work. The Reporters Committee’s Local Legal Initiative offers free legal help to local journalists, and it is a model worth scaling.

Nobody on a national desk was going to notice that two Louisville school board candidates lived on the wrong side of a new district line. Someone at Louisville Public Media did, and checked.

How we did this

Two sets of stories, built with the same rules: Farhi’s examples and the 52 2026 races that ended in a concrete consequence.

Farhi’s examples. We traced his nine national stories, two regional counter-examples and the 2022 George Santos case back to the earliest public reporting, coding what had been published before the outlet he credits ran its story.

Our review of 52 races. We collected 2026 withdrawals, disqualifications, resignations, indictments, charges and formal complaints in 33 states and at every level of office. We started with a pilot of 31, then widened the search for local TV stories, state election records, statehouse news services and ethics committees. A person had to be in a 2026 race (appointments are left out) and face a concrete result. A complaint from a partisan counts only if an agency acted on it. We left out four: a sheriff whose seat was not on the 2026 ballot, a congressional candidate whose complaint drew no agency action, and two edge cases we couldn’t cleanly place in a 2026 race, a judicial appointment and a sheriff’s resignation. All four remain in the table, flagged. We capped the top of the ballot at 40 percent so national races wouldn’t dominate the sample; the pattern below Congress doesn’t depend on that cap. We traced each story to the earliest public item in any format and only then looked at who published it. In 3 of the 43 stories below Congress, that item was the candidate’s own disclosure. We coded "who made it stick" for all 43: whether an in-state news outlet was carrying the story when the candidate withdrew, was disqualified, resigned or was charged. We count any news outlet as a newsroom, whatever its lean. One first item, Iowa Starting Line, comes from a Democratic-leaning site owned by Courier Newsroom; counting it as a party source instead would move one row and change no geography. As a sensitivity check, 10 of the 43 are weaker cases, because someone was charged but stayed in the race. Without them 33 remain, one started out of state and 15 started in the same city or county.

Limits. Starting from results means we see only stories that brought a consequence. Stories that ran and changed nothing are invisible, and so are races nobody covered. Search tools index small outlets, Facebook groups and newsletters poorly, which probably undercounts local and independent first items. Some publishers (AP, the Times, the Journal, Politico, several local papers) block our tools, so for them we relied on syndicated copies and linking outlets. We never bypassed a paywall, and some links in the table could not be checked automatically. In 7 stories we could not tell which of several same-day items came first. Several charged-but-still-running cases brought a smaller consequence than a withdrawal does; we kept them under our rule, and the pattern holds without them. Local TV had the first item in three of our 52 stories. We could not search print archives, so the Marshall check covers only what is online.
