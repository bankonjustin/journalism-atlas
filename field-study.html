The Field Has Already Reorganized

How Journalism Is Built, Funded, and Distributed in 2026
Justin Bank (Independent Journalism Atlas) · Andy Pergam (Knight Center ASU) Working draft — V10.1 · May 2026 · Not for distribution
Executive Summary

American journalism in 2026 is not a broken industry awaiting repair. It is a reorganized field — nine distinct types of journalism operators, six revenue architectures, and a set of production and distribution layers that are interoperable in ways they weren't five years ago. The integrated 20th-century newsroom has no single successor. What has replaced it does, collectively, what the newsroom used to do alone.

The funding environment has collapsed. Meta referral traffic stands at 32% of its 2020 peak. Google Search referrals to publishers fell 33% globally in a single year, with losses of 60% for small publishers. The pattern across every failure is identical: concentrated dependency leads to collapse.

Every independent journalism model hits the same ceiling. Across all nine cohorts, independent operators tend to plateau between $3.5M and $10M in annual revenue, regardless of governance structure or funding model. The ceiling is broken by only four mechanisms: B2B pricing discipline, institutional bundle mechanics, ideological capture of a dedicated audience, and organizational scaling.

The most durable response is combination. No single model is sufficient. States Newsroom produces statehouse journalism the Washington Post distributes. City Bureau coordinates civic documentation that investigative reporters use as leads. Individual creator brands maintain direct reader relationships that platforms cannot revoke. Collaborations are the future hiding in plain sight.

Two new producer classes are reshaping the field: subject matter experts fulfilling audience jobs institutional journalism structurally cannot, and ethnic/diaspora media representing the field's most significant underexploited commercial opportunity — a $45.83 billion multicultural advertising market growing at 8.4% annually, with almost no organized advertising infrastructure to capture it.

Top-Level Findings
  • There is no single successor to the integrated 20th-century newsroom. Nine distinct operator types now do collectively what it used to do alone.
  • Every independent journalism model converges toward the same revenue ceiling — $3.5M–$10M — regardless of governance structure or funding model.
  • The ceiling is broken by only four mechanisms: B2B pricing, institutional bundling, ideological audience capture, and organizational scaling.
  • Dismediation — journalism's content traveling while its economic value is captured by intermediaries who didn't produce it — is the field's defining structural threat.
  • Collaboration is the field's most powerful underutilized response. The survival rate of journalism collaboratives past year two is the most important unresolved empirical question in this study.
  • Subject Matter Expert creators are fulfilling audience jobs institutional journalism structurally cannot: trusted translation, parasocial peer access, decision support, B2B professional tooling.
  • Ethnic and diaspora media represent the field's largest commercial opportunity. URL Media and Word In Black have cracked the model. The question is who scales it.
  • AI is reorganizing the field at two levels simultaneously: as workflow infrastructure inside newsrooms, and as competitive threat to journalism's distribution economics.
  • The federated protocol layer — Bluesky ATProto, ActivityPub, Ghost 6.0 — is a directional signal, not yet a proven model. The Boston Globe documents 4.5× better subscription conversion per Bluesky follower than Threads.
Introduction

A Rapid Reorganization

The integrated 20th-century newsroom has no single successor. What has replaced it is a layered field — nine distinct types of journalism operators, using six different revenue architectures, solving different pieces of a coverage problem that no single institution can solve alone. The layers are interoperable in ways they weren't five years ago. And they are reorganizing under pressure fast enough that the field looks meaningfully different than it did when we began this research.

No single event marked the transition. It happened incrementally — through layoffs and launches, through platform withdrawals and newsletter migrations, through the quiet proliferation of nonprofit wires and civic documentation networks and creator brands built by former institutional journalists who took their audiences with them. But by 2026, the reorganization is clearly legible in what journalism operators are actually doing.

A nonprofit wire has replaced the commercial statehouse wire. States Newsroom operates 39 fully-funded state-capital newsrooms on $27.45 million in philanthropic revenue, distributing everything under Creative Commons — republished by other publishers, including the Washington Post. Civic documentation has been distributed at scale: City Bureau's Documenters Network paid community members $369,000 in stipends to cover 3,253 public meetings across 14 states in 2025, at an average cost of $113 per meeting. A new kind of portfolio conglomerate is investing in the production layer: the American Journalism Project has backed 50 local newsrooms since 2019, generating $128 million in combined portfolio revenue in 2024 — a claimed three-to-one return. And independent journalism has become structurally platform-promiscuous: Casey Newton enters his fifth year on Ghost — his third email service provider — while simultaneously licensing content to The Verge, co-hosting a podcast with the New York Times, and migrating his audience across surfaces as needed.

"The most revealing case is Roger Sollenberger. On February 15, 2026, publishing on Substack, he reported new information placing President Trump within the redacted portions of the Epstein files. Nine days later, NPR confirmed the reporting. Congressional action followed. The FBI case files were released. Sollenberger's GoFundMe raised $8,665."

The sharpest single illustration of two forces this paper keeps returning to: how trust travels in a disaggregated field, and how completely the economic infrastructure behind that trust has collapsed.

At the same moment, AI is acquiring journalism's distribution infrastructure from a different direction. On April 3, 2026, OpenAI acquired TBPN — a podcast and YouTube daily talk show that had streamed for 18 months — for a reported sum in the low hundred millions. OpenAI was not buying journalism. It was buying a production format, an audience relationship, and distribution infrastructure.

These cases don't point in the same direction. That's the point. The field has reorganized into forms that solve for different problems simultaneously — some building journalism's production and distribution capacity in new distributed forms, others dissolving its economic return. Both forces are accelerating.

This field study maps that reorganization. It identifies nine governance-based cohorts of journalism operators, documents the six revenue architectures they use, and names two structural findings that hold across all of them: that independent journalism operators tend to plateau at the same revenue ceiling regardless of model — and that the most durable response to that ceiling is combination. Collaboration. The layered architecture that does collectively what no single organization can do alone.

The collaborations are the future hiding in plain sight. This paper explains why — and what stands in the way.

I.
Context

The Funding Collapse

The context for everything that follows is a funding environment experiencing a multi-year collapse.

Bubble size approximates dollar impact. Hover for details. Events span platform withdrawal, government defunding, private equity retrenchment, and nonprofit failure.

The pattern across all of these failures is the same: concentrated dependency leads to collapse. OCCRP lost 52% of its budget to the USAID freeze in a single week. The Center for Public Integrity fell $2.5 million short of its fundraising goal and had nowhere to turn. Each organization had over-indexed on a single source — one platform, one government funder, one private-equity owner, one billionaire — and when that source moved, there was nothing to catch the fall.

In response, the market is developing more resilient, distributed, diversified funding structures. The most consequential of those responses isn't any individual business model. It's the emergence of a collaboration layer: a set of cross-outlet arrangements, shared infrastructure, and coordination networks that are beginning to do collectively what no single organization can do alone. Major investigations have become the clearest expression: the Missouri Independent, MuckRock, and AP collaborating on St. Louis radioactive contamination; States Newsroom and the New York Times Local Investigations Fellowship working ICE detention in Adams County; ProPublica's Local Reporting Network operating across four states simultaneously in 2025. These are not special projects. They are becoming the default structure for resource-intensive accountability journalism.

The Twin Forces

Collaborations are the future hiding in plain sight — building journalism's production capacity in new distributed forms. And dismediation — journalism's informational content traveling through the media field while its economic value is captured by intermediaries who did not produce it — is the threat moving in the opposite direction simultaneously. Both forces are examined in detail below.

IV.
Structural Finding

Finite Ceilings Are a Feature, Not a Bug

The most important finding in this study is not about any individual organizational model. It is about what every model runs into: a consistent revenue ceiling across all cohorts. Independent journalism operators — regardless of model, governance structure, or funding strategy — tend to plateau between approximately $3.5M and $10M in annual revenue.

Figure 4 — Defector's Five-Year Revenue Plateau
Defector is the only organization in the dataset with five consecutive years of publicly disclosed, audited annual revenue. Years 3 and 4 are identical. Year 5 subscription revenue did not grow. Jasper Wang has said explicitly: "Worker ownership is an operating and governance model, not a funding or revenue model."
Figure 5 — The Scale Ceiling Across All Cohorts
76+ US journalism organization observations. Five different organizational models — creator brands, philanthropic nonprofits, worker-owned co-ops, barbell hybrids, collaboration-layer organizations — cluster in the same $3.5M–$10M band. No single model "owns" the ceiling. This is a structural finding about information economics.

An important qualification: 23% of organizations in the full dataset operate at stable, sustainable levels below $3.5M — not as failures, but by choice or structural constraint. El Tímpano (~$2M), Aftermath ($485K), Hell Gate (below $1M until late 2025), MinnPost ($1.7M after 17 years. The thesis: independent journalism operators that attempt to scale tend to plateau in the $3.5M–$10M range. A substantial minority operate sustainably below this band, with no ceiling to break.

The Ceiling Is Broken By Four Mechanisms Only

Mechanism 1
B2B Pricing Discipline
When journalism prices information for professionals who expense it, the addressable market is fundamentally different. The decision-maker is not the consumer. Industry Dive ($525M exit), Politico Pro (~$100M), The Ankler (~$10M) are the proof points at different scales.
Mechanism 2
Institutional Bundle Mechanics
The NYT bundle captures a subscriber's entire information budget across multiple products. No single-product creator can compete for the same dollar. The Athletic integration is the clearest recent example of bundle mechanics working at the acquisition level.
Mechanism 3
Ideological Capture of a Dedicated Audience
Fox News ($16.3B Fox Corp), Daily Wire ($200M+), Turning Point USA ($85M) serve audiences with high willingness to pay and high churn resistance, because the product is identity as much as information.
Mechanism 4
Organizational Scaling
The $3.5M–$10M ceiling is partly a single-creator or small-team ceiling. Organizations that build multiple revenue streams and grow headcount move through it — States Newsroom ($27.45M), Semafor ($40M), Crooked Media (~$25–32M operating revenue).
The Uncomfortable Math

The nonprofit news sector — approximately 400 organizations — generates $650–700M in collective annual revenue: roughly 1% of what newspapers produced twenty years ago. It would take roughly 100 Defector-sized publications to offset the 2,700 journalism jobs lost in 2023 alone. The structural response is combination — not combination as editorial goodwill, but as economic architecture.

AI in Production: Infrastructure, Not Output

AI has become standard workflow infrastructure across every cohort, primarily absorbing work that previously occupied junior staff. AP's 2024 survey: nearly 70% of staff had used generative AI for social posts, newsletters, headlines, translation, or transcription. INN members: AI usage jumped from 34% to 63% in a single year. Reuters Institute 2026: 67% of publishers say AI has not yet saved any jobs despite heavy adoption. The ethic that has emerged at most institutional newsrooms is clear: AI is embedded in workflow, not output. The shift anticipated for 2026 is toward agentic AI — multi-step autonomous workflows for monitoring, document analysis, and research.

VII.
Structural Threat

Dismediation and the News Streamer

The deepest distribution problem in 2026 is not platform suppression, though that is real. The greater disruption is dismediation: the process by which journalism's informational content travels through the media field while the economic value of that journalism is captured by intermediaries who did not produce it.

A journalist produces an investigation; a social user shares a screenshot of the headline; a news streamer discusses it for forty minutes to an audience of 200,000 subscribers; a TikTok creator makes a 90-second explainer that gets five million views; an AI Overview summarizes the investigation in three sentences. Each node in this chain receives audience attention. Only the original newsroom produced the work that set the chain in motion.

The Economic Gap

Individual news streamers routinely out-earn a 20-person local newsroom's entire operating budget. Alphabet's Q3 2025 revenue: $100 billion — its first-ever $100B quarter. All bargaining-code payments globally total roughly US$350M annually. The Gao, Lee, and Murphy (2020) municipal bond research: three years after a newspaper closure, municipal bond yields rise 5.5 basis points, adding approximately $650,000 to the cost of an average $65M bond issue. Journalism's social value is measurable in financial markets. It is not captured by journalism's economic model.

VIII.
New Producer Class

The Subject Matter Expert Journalist: A Productive Thesis

In August 2022, Kyla Scanlon coined the term "vibecession" in a newsletter post. Within months, mainstream economists and Federal Reserve officials were grappling with it as a real analytical challenge. Major newspapers ran explainers based on her framing. She started as a C2 poly-platform creator and has since developed C3 barbell relationships with publishers across C1 and C5. She may be morphing from SME to journalist. It may not matter to her or her audience. It's journalism, not journalist.

This research offers a thesis, not yet a confirmed finding: these creators are not competing with journalists for the same audience job. They are fulfilling different jobs that institutional journalism structurally cannot fulfill.

Four Audience Jobs SME Creators Fill

Job 1
The Trusted-Translator Job
Complex domains — medicine, engineering, finance, AI, law — produce information that general-audience journalism systematically mistranslates. Not from bad faith, but from structural constraint. The SME creator's native domain fluency allows them to translate accurately. Jay Rosen's "view from nowhere" critique is being resolved by market forces, not institutional reform.
Job 2
The Parasocial Peer Access Job
SME creators give audiences insider access to professional worlds they cannot otherwise enter. Orosz writes about engineering culture in a way that makes a developer feel understood by a peer, not profiled by an outsider. Academic research confirms audiences extend epistemic authority to parasocial "peers" more readily than institutional authorities.
Job 3
The Decision-Support Job
Traditional journalism informs. SME creators help audiences make specific decisions. ParentData's explicit tagline: "data-driven, judgment-free decision support" — a product description, not an editorial mission. Thompson, Orosz, and Rachitsky all operate as paid intelligence for working professionals.
Job 4
The B2B Professional Tooling Job
This fourth job is economically the most important: it largely dissolves journalism's willingness-to-pay problem. The reader is not personally paying. The addressable market is fundamentally different from what journalism conventionally competes for. Thompson and Orosz compete not with the NYT, but with analytics software.

The Thesis Has a Sharp Limit

The SME creator's native domain — technology, finance, health, software engineering, economics — is not the domain of accountability journalism. Scanlon can name a vibecession; she cannot subpoena bank records. Orosz can map software engineering culture; he cannot FOIA the Department of Labor. The Sollenberger pipeline — the independent accountability journalist whose most socially valuable work generates no guaranteed economic return to its producer — remains the sharpest failure mode in the field. The SME thesis and the Sollenberger case must be held simultaneously: markets are confirming one, and markets are structurally unable to solve the other.

The Model That's Working

URL Media and Word In Black are the only two collaboration-layer organizations in this study that have built meaningful earned revenue from non-philanthropic sources. Both are identity-based networks. The demographic coherence of the audience is not incidental to the business model — it is the business model. The $45.83B multicultural advertising market overwhelmingly flows to Univision, Telemundo, and digital platforms. The question of who builds the advertising infrastructure to redirect meaningful portions of that market toward independent ethnic media is the most important unresolved commercial question in this field study.

X.
Products

News Products Across Cohorts: The Ownership Imperative

For approximately two decades, the article page was journalism's unit of commerce. That model is collapsing in real time. Google AI Overviews drove a 47% relative decline in click-through rates when they appear. Facebook referrals are at 32% of their March 2020 peak. The article page is not disappearing; it is depreciating as a commercial asset.

"The most durable journalism products are the ones where the audience relationship belongs to the journalist, not to the platform. Reach without ownership is acquisition infrastructure, not a product."

Email subscriber lists are portable. Podcast RSS feeds are portable. A direct app relationship is owned. Social followers, search traffic, and algorithmic distribution are the top of the funnel. They have never been the business. The journalism organizations most exposed in 2026 are the ones that spent the last decade optimizing for the top of the funnel and never built the owned-channel relationship that converts reach into revenue.

XI.
Conclusion

Decentralization and What Comes Next

The research began with a thesis: concentrated dependency leads to failure. The rational — and now market-driven — response has been distributed, diversified structures.

The most durable revenue pattern is subscription-primary with one or two diversified secondary streams, and no single source above 50% of revenue. Tangle (85%/15%). The Dispatch (85%/10%/5%). Defector (82% subscription plus four secondaries). Texas Tribune (25%/24%/19%/18%). In each case, diversification is not an accident of success — it is the strategy.

The distribution corollary: distributed, owned-first audience relationships are the resilience model against platform dependency. Email addresses are portable. Podcast subscribers acquired through a direct website relationship are portable. Platform followers are borrowed from a platform that may change its terms, its algorithm, or its existence.

Collaboration is the structural response — not collaboration as editorial goodwill, but as economic architecture: the production layer, coordination layer, and distribution layer each handled by the form best suited to it. States Newsroom handles statehouse production. ICIJ handles cross-border investigation. City Bureau handles civic documentation. INN's NewsMatch handles philanthropic infrastructure. Individual creator brands handle direct reader relationships and niche depth. These layers are interoperable in a way they were not five years ago. A Documenters note becomes a States Newsroom reporter's lead, which gets republished by the Washington Post, which feeds a creator's newsletter. No single institution designed this. It emerged from the field's reorganization under pressure.

"The collaborations are the future that has been hiding in plain sight."

Open Questions

What the Research Cannot Yet Answer

  • Collaborative survival rates past year two
    Stefanie Murray at the Center for Cooperative Media has the data — more than 1,160 collaborative journalism projects tracked since 2018. Murray's database is the only systematic source tracking collaboration survival rates at this scale. This is the paper's most important empirical gap. [Priority interview: Collaborative Journalism Summit, May 2026.]
  • The SME thesis, tested
    Do SME creators understand themselves as fulfilling distinct audience jobs? Do those jobs map onto willingness to pay? The four-job framework is a structural inference from economic and audience data — it needs practitioner confirmation or correction.
  • The news streamer dependency
    Does any news streamer have a disclosed view on their dependency on journalism production they don't fund? The Tenet Media indictment opened a crack in the economics of political creator content.
  • CPB dissolution long-term
    Does it accelerate consolidation of public-media spectrum into commercial ownership, or reset around state and local funding models? The NJ Civic Information Consortium is the only functioning evidence of what a state-level architecture looks like.
  • The federated distribution horizon
    Ghost ActivityPub is live and in the field; Bluesky is functionally centralized despite its architecture. When does protocol-based distribution become a journalism use case at scale, rather than a practitioner signal? [ATmosphere May 2026 produced practitioner evidence to incorporate here.]
  • Ethnic media economic infrastructure
    Multicultural advertising spending reached $45.83B in 2024. Almost none flows to independent ethnic media. URL Media and Word In Black have cracked the model. Who scales it?
  • The Dispatch FY2025 watch
    The Dispatch at ~$5.5M (FY2024) is targeting $10M for 2025 via organizational scaling: SCOTUSblog acquisition, new verticals, B2B professional tier development. If it reaches $10M on a subscription-primary model, it confirms ORG_SCALING as the fourth ceiling-breaking mechanism working at sub-100-staff scale.
