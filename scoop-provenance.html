What the data shows

Below the top of the ballot (state legislatures, school boards, sheriffs, DAs, judges, cities and counties), every first report with a named source came from inside the state, and about half from the same city or county. National outlets appear only at the top of the ballot.

Phase 1: Farhi’s examples, traced

In a Poynter column, Paul Farhi argued that national outlets landed the biggest stories of the 2026 campaign. Before building the consequence set, we traced his own examples: nine Senate and House stories he credits to national outlets, two regional counter-examples, and the 2022 George Santos case as a historical control. For each, we coded who published the core allegation first, who published the decisive story, and whether a local or state outlet had published the allegation or the underlying facts earlier.

Corrections from tracing

  • Platner texts (S01). The Wall Street Journal, not the Times, established the core fact first, on the same day. Farhi’s Maine Wire item ran after both and was about something else.
  • Mills (S09). The Daytona Beach News-Journal printed a rival’s specific stolen-valor accusations in August 2024, before NOTUS. That item leaned toward validating Mills’s medals, so we flagged the call.
  • Platner source (S02). Fall 2025 Jewish Insider and CNN pieces described the source only as an “acquaintance” and reported nothing about the relationship, so we found no earlier antecedent.
  • Marshall (S07). We found no earlier Kansas coverage, but we could not search the print archives of the Great Bend Tribune or the Hutchinson News for 2003 to 2020. This check is still open.
  • Santos (S12). The North Shore Leader’s earlier reporting is documented online from October 31, 2022. We could not verify a September 2022 print date.
  • Swalwell (S10). The San Francisco Chronicle’s April 10 story was the decisive report, and CNN published an allegation from the same source the same day. CNN’s April 7 piece, which reported the campaign’s denial of a “rumor” of misconduct without specifying an allegation, is coded a tier B antecedent. It is not an earlier report of the allegation.
  • Attributions cut both ways. Correcting Farhi’s credit hands the story to another national outlet in S01 (WSJ) and S08 (TMZ). It goes to a local outlet only in S09. On federal races his case mostly holds.

The Phase 1 coding is a seed set of 12 stories chosen by Farhi’s column. Nothing here generalizes beyond them.

Methods

Consequence first

We started from outcomes, not outlets. We collected 2026 races, at every level of office, where an accountability story was followed by something concrete: a withdrawal or suspended campaign, a court disqualification, a resignation, an indictment, or a formal ethics complaint or investigation. We then traced each event to its earliest public report in any format, and only then looked at who published it.

Tiers

When we traced earlier reporting, we coded each antecedent by tier:

  • A, same allegation. An earlier publication reported the core allegation or fact pattern.
  • B, adjacent fact. An earlier publication reported a material underlying fact, record or relationship the decisive story built on.
  • C, record only. The underlying public record existed, but no outlet reported it.
  • D, none found. A documented search found no antecedent.

A specific allegation printed even as a rumor counts as tier A. Vague misconduct is tier B. A pre-emptive denial alone is not an antecedent.

The 40% cap

Federal and statewide races were capped at 40% of the set so the rest of the ballot could not be crowded out. In this set they are 11 of 31.

Review rulings applied

  • Appointments and resignations from seats not on the ballot are included and flagged.
  • Self-disclosure is its own trigger type, not a press win.
  • A complaint filed by a partisan counts as a consequence only if an agency acted on it. One candidate event that failed this test was removed from the set. One that passed because agencies reviewed it, though they dismissed it, is kept and flagged.
  • Where the earliest item turned out to be a different outlet from the first coding, we corrected it (for example, the Iowa and Illinois events).

How we classified first reports

For the source chart, a newsroom is any news outlet, including a blog or a newsletter, whatever its editorial lean. Iowa Starting Line, a Democratic-leaning site, counts as a newsroom. An official record is a court or agency filing not made by a partisan. Partisan means a PAC, party, opponent, or a complaint filed by a partisan. Citizen means the candidate or a private person, including an influencer post.

Limits

Starting from consequences means we see only stories that changed something. Stories that ran and changed nothing are invisible, and so are races nobody covered. Search tools index small outlets, Facebook groups and newsletters poorly, which probably undercounts local and independent first reports. Some publishers, including the New York Times, Wall Street Journal and Politico, could not be opened by our tools, so we relied on linking outlets and syndicated copies for them. We never bypassed a paywall. Print archives were not searched. 15 of the 31 events are disputed and 6 are in litigation; we describe them that way.
